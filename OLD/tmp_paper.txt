Characterisation of Aggregated Electric Thermostatic Loads
and Quantification of its Flexibility
Alexandre M. V. Gouveia, Md. Umar Hashmi, Reinhilde D’hulst, Dirk Van Hertem

Abstract—Distribution system operators (DSOs) rarely know how
many electric thermostatic loads (ETLs), such as heat pumps, are connected to their low-voltage networks. This paper characterises aggregated
ETL populations by estimating their installed capacity, temperaturedriven energy and flexibility potential, using a net-load and simultaneity
factor (SF) fit. The net-load fit characterises the ETL response and
gives its aggregated temperature-sensitive demand; the SF converts that
demand into the summed non-coincident installed peak. The SF fit is
stable against a population’s ETL rate and installed capacity, and its
simultaneity sensitivity transfers across the two populations studied.
Therefore, a curve calibrated on a submetered pilot serves that population’s un-submetered feeders. Across aggregated Swiss smart meters, the
installed capacity is recovered to a weighted absolute percentage error of
10%. On a real German feeder held out of sample, the estimate reaches
7% once the threshold temperature is read from the observable net-load
fit. After calibration on a submetered pilot, applying the method to a
target feeder requires only net load and ambient temperature, making
the method suitable for aggregated ETL quantification in unobservable
low-voltage networks.
Index Terms—Electric thermostatic loads, heat pumps, low-voltage
networks, simultaneity factor, installed capacity, smart meter data,
distribution system observability.

The electrification of residential heating is a central lever of
decarbonisation policy, and the heat pump (HP) is its main instrument.
Its uptake is growing fast [1]. With it grows the share of household
demand that follows outdoor temperature, not occupancy alone. On
200 German LV feeders, installed HP power rose by over twenty per
cent from 2023 to 2025 [2].
This uptake supports decarbonisation, yet it loads low-voltage (LV)
networks that were not sized for it. Field and simulation studies show
HPs raise coincident feeder and transformer peaks, sharpest in cold
weather, alongside electric vehicles and rooftop solar [3]–[7]. To plan
and operate these networks, network operators must know how much
thermostatic load sits behind each metering point.
A distribution system operator (DSO) rarely holds that information.
It meters aggregated net load and can obtain weather. It seldom holds
the consumer count, appliance registers or nameplate ratings needed
to interpret the thermal response [2], [8]. The installed capacity of
the electric thermostatic loads (ETLs) behind a feeder therefore stays
largely unobserved.
Four lines of research attempt to close this gap. Non-intrusive load
monitoring and HP detection recover thermostatic load from customer
meters, either as a present-absent label [9]–[11] or as a disaggregated
energy time series [12], [13]. Some extend to LV substations [14],
[15]. Yet these methods stop short of the installed capacity, and their
substation demonstrations remain largely synthetic.
Change-point and degree-day models fit load as a piecewiselinear function of temperature. They range from canonical buildinglevel formulations [16]–[18], reviewed in [19], to aggregated and
smart-meter populations [20]–[22]. They recover heating and cooling
sensitivities and a threshold temperature, but stop at the coincident
response and never reach the non-coincident installed sum.
For photovoltaics (PV), by contrast, installed-capacity estimation
is well developed. Methods infer the capacity from aggregated net
load or smart-meter data [23]–[26], and even recover the servicetransformer rating this way [27]. The thermostatic-load counterpart
is largely missing [8].
The simultaneity of an aggregated HP population, and the coincident peak it produces, inform network planning: they set the design
load [28]–[31] and bound the flexibility the population offers [32],
[33]. Planners measure these quantities on submetered populations of
known size and read them as a planning input, rather than recover
them from an un-submetered feeder.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no prior method estimates
the installed capacity of an aggregated ETL population directly from
feeder-level net load without submetering the target feeder. This paper
addresses that gap. The contributions are threefold.
1) A bathtub curve, fitted to the aggregated net load against
ambient temperature, characterises the ETLs behind a metering
point without any submetering. Its two arms recover the ETL
temperature response, its thresholds mark where the population
heats and cools, and its flat centre isolates the temperatureindependent load. Integrating the heating arm over the year
then estimates the temperature-driven ETL energy consumption
from data the DSO already holds.

The fitted parameters carry a physical reading. The threshold
temperatures Th and Tc are behavioural: they mark where the
population, on average, begins to heat or to cool, rather than a fixed
property of any dwelling. Below Th a heating load appears and above
Tc a cooling load appears, each growing close to linearly with the
distance from its threshold. Between the two lies a dead band where
the population neither heats nor cools.
The slopes sh and sc measure how much load the population
adds per degree beyond each threshold. A steeper arm signals more
installed ETLs. The sensitivity therefore also carries the ETL rate
(the share of consumers with an ETL), since a feeder with more heat
pumps shows a steeper heating arm, all else equal. Section V-B confirms this, with sh tracking installed capacity across the substations.
The flat centre Pbase is the temperature-independent load. It is not
purely non-ETL, as it also absorbs the part of the ETL that runs
regardless of weather, such as domestic water heating. The base term
thus slightly overstates the non-ETL load, and the arms miss this
weather-independent share of the ETL, which is out of scope for this
paper.
The two slopes together characterise a feeder. Where heating
dominates, sc is near zero and the bathtub reduces to a heating
hockey stick. Where cooling dominates, sh is near zero. The Swiss
and German feeders studied here are heating-dominated, whereas the
mixed-climate aggregate of Fig. 2 from Austin shows both arms. The
balance of the two slopes varies from region to region with climate,

building stock and the dominant ETL type. The fitted response is
therefore a compact signature of a network’s thermal make-up.

III. E NERGY AND I NSTALLED C APACITY E STIMATION

The two functions of Section II estimate three quantities a DSO
needs about the ETL population: its energy consumption, installed
capacity and flexibility potential. Energy consumption follows from
the net-load function alone. Installed capacity and flexibility also need
the SF function, since the net load exposes the aggregated ETL load,
not the non-coincident sum of the individual peaks. Section IV covers
flexibility; each use case is a relation between the fitted parameters,
evaluated in Section V.

from its baseline consumption without breaching indoor thermal

bands [38]. Over this ∆tT h , the aggregated HPs must deliver the
temperature-set energy demand. This is the temperature-driven ETL

V. C ASE S TUDIES
A. Dataset and Substation Design
The data combines two Swiss sources, the HEAPO dataset [36]
and an additional smart-meter dataset [37], pooled into one set of LV
consumers. The ETL used throughout is the HP. Other ETLs appear
in the dataset. They are only flagged, not submetered, so the analysis
withholds them. The load profiles have 15-minute resolution; ambient
temperature is hourly. One hour is therefore the finest resolution for
the fits.
Synthetic substations come from this pool by iterating over a size
and ETL-rate grid. The consumer count N and the HP count Nhp
are each capped at 50. Every feasible cell of the grid, with Nhp ≤
N , holds the same number of replicates, giving 1000 substations.
Two independent samples with replacement draw the HPs and the
base households, which decouples the HP count from the household
composition. Each substation belongs to a single weather station, so
one temperature series applies to all its members.

Averaging the load over longer windows removes the occupancy

Spatial aggregation acts the same way, as more meters average out
the load that is not thermal. At daily resolution the net-load fit rises
from a median R2 of 0.92 behind ten consumers to 0.96 behind fifty.
It climbs more steeply with the HPs whose signal it recovers, from
0.88 at five HPs to 0.97 at fifty. The SF fit, built from the HPs alone,

Equation (8) needs only the net-load-fitted function. The heating
arm, integrated over the year, gives the temperature-driven HP energy,
compared against the substation’s own submetered HP load. Across
the 1000 substations the estimate tracks the submetered energy with
a Pearson correlation ρ of 0.99, but underestimates it by a median
12% (a median ratio of 0.88) at a WAPE of 15%.
The energy estimator has two resolutions: one for the net-load fit
and one for the temperature integration. Table III reports the WAPE
and the median estimated-to-actual energy ratio, which falls below
one when the estimate runs low, for each combination. Integrating
the daily-fitted arm over the hourly temperature series, rather than
the daily mean, lifts the ratio from 0.88 to 0.92 and cuts the WAPE
from 15% to 12%. A day whose temperature crosses the threshold
holds more heating-degree-hours than its single-point mean implies.
Refitting the arm at hourly resolution instead degrades the estimate,
to a ratio of 0.86, since the sub-daily load is noisier than its daily
mean (Section V-B). The daily fit integrated over the finer temperature
series is therefore the best combination.
The estimate is temperature-driven by construction, so the dead
band bounds it. About 7% of the HP energy falls above the threshold
temperature, in mild weather where the heating arm is zero, most
plausibly domestic hot water [12]. Measured against this temperaturedriven share alone, on the days below the threshold temperature, the
estimate tightens to a WAPE of 10% at a ratio of 0.94. Equation (8)
therefore estimates the temperature-driven energy rather than the
annual total, the dead-band limit anticipated in Section III-A.
Aggregation acts on the spread of the error, not its centre. Fig. 8
shows the signed error across substations, right-skewed with a median

that offsets it at a low ETL rate fades. More HPs therefore sharpen
the estimate around a biased centre rather than removing the bias.

G. Generalisation to a Real Unseen Feeder
The Swiss results rest on synthetic aggregates of one population.
A stronger test applies the method to a real aggregate from another
country, summed from field meters and never seen in any fit. This
aggregate comes from the WPuQ measurement campaign [40]: its
net load is the sum of 37 individually metered German single-family
houses, each with a submetered HP, giving a measured installed HP
capacity of 239 kW. The net-load fit (Section II-A) uses the feeder’s
own metered load, and the SF fit (Section II-B) transfers from the
Swiss reference curve of Section V-C. Submetered data serves only
to validate the results, never as input.
Both fitted functions carry to the real feeder (Fig. 11). The net-load
hockey stick fits at an R2 of 0.84, close to the Swiss median, and
places the threshold temperature at 13.9 ◦ C. The method transfers
only the SF slope from the Swiss reference and anchors it at this
observable threshold, so the feeder’s own SF serves only to validate.
That validation fit keeps the same shape and slope, though noisier on
a single aggregate, at a below-threshold R2 of 0.72. The forms are
therefore not an artefact of the synthetic design.
The transfer turns on the slope alone, because the threshold is read
locally by construction. The feeder’s simultaneity sensitivity, about
0.016 ◦ C−1 , sits within the spread of the Swiss fits (median 0.0161,
interquartile range 0.015–0.017; Table I), so the slope holds across
the two populations. The threshold temperature would not carry: the
feeder’s SF saturates near 13 ◦ C against the Swiss 16 ◦ C, a milderclimate shift of about three degrees, as the balance-point temperature
varies with building fabric, internal gains and setpoint preference
rather than a fixed constant [41]. Reading it from the net-load fit
sidesteps this shift, since that fit places the threshold at 13.9 ◦ C
and, across the Swiss substations, the net-load and SF threshold
temperatures coincide to a median of 0.1 ◦ C. At the feeder’s coldest
day, near −5 ◦ C, the transferred curve then reads an SF of 0.34
against the true 0.32.

threshold and capacity, it gives 6.9 MWh of annual flexible energy
per hour of window against an actual 6.5 MWh, a ratio of 1.07. Here
the flexible-energy overestimate and the capacity underestimate carry
opposite signs: the transferred SF overstates the feeder’s SF(1 − SF)

potential remains an idealisation. It takes the square wave as feasible
and ignores each dwelling’s thermal state and comfort band, so it

∆tT h must come from the building stock’s thermal inertia, not the
fits.

VI. D ISCUSSION
A. Accuracy
The capacity estimate carries two error sources, both traced in the
results. The non-ETL temperature response that Equation (1) neglects
biases the net-load numerator. At a low ETL rate this occupancydriven load inflates the heating slope. The energy estimate then turns
from a 15% underestimate at full count to an overestimate as the
HPs thin out (Table V). A zero-HP control makes this concrete:
aggregating only HP-free households, the net-load fit still shows
a small heating slope, and Equation (9) infers about 0.37 kW of
spurious capacity per household where no HP exists. This floor is
small per home but grows with feeder size, so it dominates the
estimate at a low ETL rate and is negligible once HPs supply most of
the temperature-driven load. The SF denominator carries the transfer

B. Generalisation
The SF generalises in a specific and testable way. Within a
population it is empirically stable against ETL rate and installed
capacity over the tested range, so a curve from a submetered pilot
serves that population’s un-submetered feeders. Across populations
the sensitivity m transfers, at 0.0161 against 0.0162 ◦ C−1 between
the Swiss and German data, while the threshold temperature follows
the local climate. The net-load fit supplies that threshold temperature
from data the DSO already meters, and the base fraction is negligible,
so the only quantity that need travel between populations is the slope
m. Generalisation therefore reduces to transferring a single parameter,
and the SF need not be re-derived in full for each network. This
mirrors the inverse problem solved for PV installed capacity against
irradiance [26], here posed against ambient temperature.
C. Data Requirements
The two functions ask for different data. The net-load function
needs only the aggregated net load and ambient temperature, both
already held by the DSO, and runs on any feeder. The SF function
needs submetered ETL load and installed capacity, which the DSO
rarely holds. A single submetered pilot of the same population
calibrates it, and it then transfers, so no feeder under study is
submetered. A pilot of a few tens of HPs suffices, since the SF has
settled by then (Table I).
D. Limitations
Several limits temper the results. The submetered pool holds
about fifty distinct HPs on one weather station, so the synthetic
substations reuse households and the train and test groups share
them. A household-disjoint split narrows this concern, reproducing
the transferred SF shape at a median R2 of 0.88 within the population
(Section V-C). The cross-population generalisation, however, still

rests on the single real feeder, one real aggregate rather than a
population of feeders. The model neglects the non-ETL temperature
response throughout, which the low-ETL-rate bias exposes.
A further confounder is deliberate control. Swiss and German
domestic HPs commonly run under interruptible tariffs with defined
blocking periods, and since January 2024 §14a EnWG lets German
DSOs throttle HPs above 4.2 kW [42]. Such control truncates the aggregated ETL peak. It biases the net-load numerator and the capacity
estimate downwards, consistent with the German underestimate, and
imprints regime-specific structure that a single-slope transfer need not
capture. The WPuQ campaign predates §14a, but blocking-period
tariffs do not. Underlying all of these is the broader shortage of
submetered ETL data, which a wider pilot would relieve.
E. Concluding Remarks
The method trades a modest, characterised error for a large gain in
deployability. Where a submetered pilot of the population exists, the
installed capacity of an un-submetered feeder follows to within about
ten percent, and its energy and flexibility from the same fits. Where
it does not, the net-load function alone still bounds the temperaturedriven energy. The estimates are least reliable on feeders with a
handful of HPs and most reliable once tens aggregate, which is the
regime a substation presents.
VII. C ONCLUSION
This paper presented a method to quantify an aggregated ETL
from data available to DSOs. Two temperature-response functions
were fitted, an unsupervised net-load bathtub and a supervised SF,
and three planning quantities were derived from them: installed
capacity, temperature-driven energy and a flexible-energy potential.
The SF proved empirically stable against ETL rate and installed
capacity within a population, and its sensitivity transferred across
the two populations studied, which lets a submetered pilot serve unsubmetered feeders. Across Swiss substations resampled from one
pool, the method recovered the installed capacity to a WAPE of 10%.
On a single held-out German feeder it reached 7% under the hybrid
procedure, which reads the threshold temperature from the observable
net-load fit, or 17% with the SF transferred whole.

10

Regarding future work, three directions follow. The submetered
pool was small and drawn from one region, so a wider and more
diverse set of metered HPs would test the transfer on genuinely
independent populations and firm up the single transferable sensitivity. Future work will evaluate data-driven methods for HP and
ETL quantification against the physical estimator, to see where a
learned model earns its data cost and where the two-parameter fit
suffices. Finally, it will address the dead-band limit by modelling
the non-temperature-driven ETL consumption, chiefly domestic hot
water and baseline operation. This consumption is what the heating
arm omits, and it sets the gap between the temperature-driven and
the annual energy.
