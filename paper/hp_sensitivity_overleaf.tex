\title{Estimating the Installed Capacity of Aggregated Thermostatically Controlled Loads from Net Load and Temperature}

\author{Alexandre~M.~V.~Gouveia, Md. Umar Hashmi, Reinhilde D'hulst, Dirk Van Hertem}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The electrification of heating and cooling is connecting more thermostatically controlled loads (TCLs), such as heat pumps (HPs), to low-voltage networks. However, distribution system operators (DSOs) rarely know the installed capacity of the TCLs behind each substation or feeder. This paper estimates that capacity from the aggregated net load and ambient temperature that DSOs already hold. The installed capacity follows as the ratio of two temperature sensitivities: that of a piecewise-linear net-load fit, and that of a simultaneity factor (SF) fit calibrated on a submetered pilot. Only the SF sensitivity is transferred; each aggregate keeps the threshold temperature of its own net-load fit. On 1260 semi-synthetic aggregates of Swiss households, the capacity is recovered at a weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE) of 19\%, with the error growing as the share of households with an HP falls. On three real Swiss and German aggregates outside the pilot, where every household has an HP, the estimates lie within 10\% of the submetered capacity, against up to 26\% when the full SF curve is transferred. The temperature-driven HP energy needs no pilot and is recovered at a WAPE of 9\%, and a flexibility potential derived from both fits at a WAPE of 10\% against its submetered reference. Across populations from five countries, the SF sensitivity transfers within 12\% inside one climate zone. It differs by 13--14\% between HP technologies, however, and up to 1.7-fold between climate zones and between social and private housing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The electrification of residential heating and cooling is a central lever of decarbonisation policy. Its instruments are thermostatically controlled loads (TCLs), such as heat pumps (HPs), electric resistance heaters and air conditioners, and the HP leads their uptake \cite{Iea22}. With them grows the share of household demand that follows outdoor temperature. On 200 German low-voltage (LV) feeders, for example, installed HP power rose by over twenty per cent from 2023 to 2025 \cite{Tre26}.

This uptake supports decarbonisation, yet it loads LV networks that were not sized for it. Field and simulation studies show that TCLs raise coincident feeder and transformer peaks in extreme weather. For HPs, the most studied case, these peaks are sharpest in cold weather and add to those of electric vehicles and rooftop solar \cite{Vel11, Akm14, Som21, Bec24, Lia26}. To plan and operate these networks, network operators must know how much thermostatic load sits behind each metering point.

A distribution system operator (DSO) rarely holds that information. It meters aggregated net load and can obtain weather data. It usually knows how many consumers each feeder serves, but seldom holds the appliance registers or nameplate ratings needed to interpret the thermal response \cite{Gou26, Tre26}. The installed capacity of the TCLs behind a feeder therefore stays largely unobserved.

\chg{Installed capacity, rather than the coincident peak, is the quantity sought here, for three reasons. It scales with adoption, so it supports planning scenarios as the number of TCLs grows. It bounds the peak the population can draw when its devices are synchronised, for instance after a tariff blocking period, in which the utility interrupts the HP supply, or during the activation of flexibility. Finally, multiplied by the simultaneity factor (SF) used in planning, i.e. the share of the installed capacity drawn at once, it gives the design peak that sizes transformers and cables.}

\input{tables/tab_litreview}

\chg{Five lines} of research approach this gap, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:litreview}. Non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM) and HP detection recover thermostatic load from customer meters, either as a present-absent label \cite{Fei13, Wei20b, Ray19} or as a disaggregated energy time series \cite{Bru23, Gis26}. Some extend to LV substations \cite{Wan20, Oma24}. These methods, however, need labelled or submetered training data, stop short of the installed capacity, and remain largely synthetic at the substation.

Change-point and degree-day models fit load as a piecewise-linear function of temperature. They range from canonical building-level formulations \cite{Fel08, Ruc92, Kis04}, reviewed in \cite{Faz16}, to aggregated and smart-meter populations \cite{Ali11, Per17b, Mac19}. They recover heating and cooling sensitivities and a threshold temperature without any labels. They stop, however, at the coincident response and never reach the non-coincident installed sum.

For photovoltaics (PV) and other distributed energy resources (DERs), by contrast, installed-capacity estimation is well developed. Methods infer the capacity from aggregated net load or smart-meter data \cite{Was21, Liu22, Gou25, gouveia2026pvcapacity}, and even recover the service-transformer rating this way \cite{Azz24}. Irradiance plays there the role that temperature could play for TCLs, yet the thermostatic-load counterpart is largely missing \cite{Gou26}.

The simultaneity of an aggregated TCL population, and the coincident peak it produces, inform network planning, as studied mostly for HPs. They set the design load \cite{Lov17, Che21b, Rou23} and bound the flexibility the population offers \cite{Mul19, Zir19, Dhu15}. \chg{Closest to this paper, Anderson et al.\ \cite{And20} fit a probabilistic model of HP demand against temperature to submetered customer data, and use it to assess LV networks under a given HP penetration. Such models run in the forward direction, from a known number of HPs to the demand they cause.} Planners measure these quantities on submetered populations of known size, rather than recover them from an un-submetered feeder.

\chg{A separate body of work models aggregated TCL populations for control. A population can be described through the thermal parameters of its devices, either to deliver load following \cite{Cal09} or as a stochastic battery whose power and energy limits bound its flexibility \cite{Hao15}. The state of such a population can be estimated from its aggregate power \cite{Mat13}, and the air-conditioning demand of a feeder can be disaggregated in real time from feeder measurements \cite{Led18}. These methods, however, assume known device models or learn them from building- and device-level data, and they work at control time scales. None of them addresses the planning question of how much TCL capacity is installed behind the meter.}

To the best of the authors' knowledge, no prior method estimates the installed capacity of an aggregated TCL population from feeder-level net load without submetering the target feeder. The change-point models observe the right signal but lack the step from coincident demand to installed capacity, while the simultaneity studies hold that step but only on submetered populations. \chg{The TCL aggregate models, in turn, assume the population is already known. This paper joins the first two.} The contributions are threefold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Estimation of the installed TCL capacity behind a feeder from its net load and temperature, without submetering the feeder. Established change-point models give the slope with which the daily net load rises below a balance-point temperature \cite{Faz16}. This paper adds the step from that coincident demand to installed capacity, by dividing it by the slope of the SF calibrated on a small set of submetered households. Each feeder keeps its own balance point, which follows its building stock and climate.
\item Evidence on when the SF slope transfers between groups of households. Within 50 submetered Swiss HPs, it is stable against the number of HPs and their capacity. On 1260 aggregates built from measured Swiss households, the capacity error is quantified against HP penetration, aggregate size and data resolution. On three real Swiss and German aggregates, transferring the slope alone keeps the error within 10\%, against up to 26\% for the full SF curve. Across submetered households in five countries, the slope transfers within one climate zone, but not between climate zones or between social and private housing.
\item Estimation of the temperature-driven TCL energy and of a flexibility potential from the same two fits. The energy follows from the net-load slope alone, so it needs no submetered households. The flexibility is the energy the TCLs could shift within a window allowed by building thermal inertia, while delivering the same total energy. It equals the temperature-driven load times the unused share of capacity, one minus the SF, and is compared with the same quantity computed from hourly submetered data.
\end{enumerate}

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the problem, defines the net-load and SF fits and characterises them across datasets; Section~\ref{sec:estimation} derives the energy, installed capacity and flexibility; Section~\ref{sec:results} evaluates them on semi-synthetic aggregates of one Swiss population; Section~\ref{sec:validation} applies the method to real Swiss and German aggregates outside the pilot; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses accuracy, generalisation and data requirements. Lastly, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Problem and Approach}
\label{sec:problem}
\label{sec:fits}

Privacy concerns and limits on metering and data exchange leave DSOs without full observability of individual consumers. Their records of DERs also lag behind what is actually connected. DERs that consume or generate with a high simultaneity factor matter most for reliable operation, since they add to the peak together. This work therefore uses the load profiles a DSO does hold, aggregated in space at the feeder head or substation and in time over the day. From these, it estimates the installed capacity and flexibility potential of the thermostatic loads behind the meter.

A metered point of an LV network, such as a substation or feeder, aggregates the load of the consumers downstream. This paper calls such a metered point, together with the consumers behind it, an aggregate. Some of its consumers run TCLs. The task is to quantify those TCLs from the aggregate's net load.

The net load is the sum of a TCL and a non-TCL component,
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{net}}(t) = P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(t) + P_{\text{non-TCL}}(t),
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
where only the TCL component responds directly to temperature. The non-TCL component also carries a weaker, indirect temperature dependence, visible in the whole-home energy signature \cite{Wes20}. Still, TCLs dominate the total temperature response, so the model neglects the non-TCL contribution. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} revisits this assumption.

The problem is to recover the aggregated TCL load $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(t)$ from the aggregated net load. From it follow three quantities that aid DSO operation and planning: the TCLs' installed capacity, their energy consumption and their flexibility potential. Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} sketches the pipeline.

Two fits describe the TCL population's response to ambient temperature. The first, the net-load fit, uses data the DSO already holds, with no submetering. The second, the SF fit, needs submetered TCL data. Both share the same piecewise-linear form and use daily means, which reduce the effect of building thermal inertia \cite{Per17b}.

\subsection{Net-Load Fit}
\label{sec:fits-net}


When an aggregate's net load is averaged over each day and plotted against the daily mean temperature, it follows a piecewise-linear pattern. The load is roughly flat on mild days, and rises close to linearly as the temperature falls below, or rises above, a threshold.
%(ADD the table here we discussed)
 The net-load fit expresses the aggregated net load of \eqref{eq:decomp} as
\begin{equation}
\hat{P}_{\mathrm{net}}(T) = P_{\mathrm{base}} + s_h \max(0, T_h - T) + s_c \max(0, T - T_c),
\label{eq:bathtub}
\end{equation}
where $P_{\mathrm{base}}$ is the temperature-independent load [kW], $s_h$ and $s_c$ are the heating and cooling sensitivities [kW/$^\circ$C], and $T_h \le T_c$ are the heating and cooling threshold temperatures [$^\circ$C], also known as balance-point temperatures, bounding a dead band over which no thermal response is present. The parameters of the fitted curve are computed through Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), with $T_h$ and $T_c$ being the values that minimise the squared residuals of the entire fit. The two temperature-dependent segments, called the heating and cooling arms of the fit, form the fitted aggregated TCL load.

%\textcolor{red}{should mention that $P_{\mathrm{base}}, s_h, s_c$ are the outcome of least square fit}

Note that \eqref{eq:bathtub} needs only the net load and ambient temperature the DSO already holds, and no submetered or labelled ground truth. Fig.~\ref{fig:bathtub} shows the net-load fit to aggregated Austin smart-meter data \cite{pecanstreet}, with the parameters of \eqref{eq:bathtub} marked. The thresholds divide the temperature axis into three regimes. R1 (below $T_h$) and R3 (above $T_c$) are the heating and cooling regimes, and R2 is the dead band between them, where the population neither heats nor cools. This work characterises R1 and R3 and treats R2 as out of scope.

\begin{equation}
\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\,i}(T) = s_h^{\,i}\,\max(0, T_h^{\,i} - T) + s_c^{\,i}\,\max(0, T - T_c^{\,i}),
\label{eq:tclfit}
\end{equation}

The fitted parameters carry a physical interpretation. The threshold temperatures $T_h$ and $T_c$ are behavioural. They mark where the population, on average, begins to heat or cool, rather than a fixed property of any building. Below $T_h$ a heating load appears and above $T_c$ a cooling load appears, each growing close to linearly with the distance from its threshold. Between the two lies a dead band where the population neither heats nor cools.
Individual households start heating or cooling at different temperatures. Once many are aggregated, these differences average out, and $T_h$ and $T_c$ settle at values that reflect regional preferences for starting space heating or cooling (Section~\ref{sec:cross}).

The sensitivities $s_h$ and $s_c$ of the net-load fit measure how much heating or cooling load the population adds per degree.
% A steeper arm signals more installed TCLs.
They scale with the TCL capacity installed behind the meter, so an aggregate with more TCLs shows a steeper arm, all else equal. Between aggregates of the same size, a steeper arm therefore also indicates a higher TCL penetration, i.e., a larger share of consumers with a TCL. Section~\ref{sec:res-ff} confirms this, with $s_h$ tracking installed capacity across the aggregates.

The flat centre $P_{\mathrm{base}}$ is the load that remains on days between $T_h$ and $T_c$. The net load on these days is not constant, but it shows no trend with temperature. This temperature-independent load is outside the scope of this paper.

The balance between $s_h$ and $s_c$ reflects the local climate. Hot regions are expected to be cooling-dominated, with $s_h \approx 0$, whereas regions with long winters, such as the Nordic countries, are heating-dominated, with $s_c \approx 0$. \subsection{Simultaneity-Factor Fit}
\label{sec:fits-sf}

% \textcolor{red}{
% What is simultaneity factor?
% why is it important?
% }


The SF, also called the coincidence factor, is the ratio of the demand of a group of loads, here TCLs, at a given instant to the sum of their individual maximum demands. It reflects that connected devices do not all draw their full power at the same instant \cite{fani2024impact}. In distribution networks, the SF is central to planning. It lets utilities size transformers, cables and protection for the peak the group actually draws, rather than for the sum of individual peaks.


% The second function characterises the synchronous behaviour, or simultaneity, of the TCL population.
Mathematically, the SF is the fraction of the aggregate's installed capacity consumed at a given temperature $T$, and is given as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SF}(T) = \frac{P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)}{P_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\max}},
\label{eq:sf}
\end{equation}
where $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)$ is the daily mean TCL load on a day of mean temperature $T$, and $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\max}$ is the cumulative installed capacity of all the TCLs in the aggregate. In this work, the installed capacity of each TCL is taken as the 99.9th percentile of its submetered 15-min draw. The installed capacity of an aggregate is thus the summed peak electrical draw of its TCLs, which tracks their rated power while ignoring isolated metering spikes.

\chg{Since $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)$ is a daily mean, the SF of this paper is a daily-mean SF. It is lower than the coincidence factor used in network planning, which takes the peak interval. On the coldest day of the Kloten data, the 15-min peak SF of the 50 submetered HPs is 1.28 times the daily mean, and on a median heating day 1.75 times. The daily mean suits the aim of this paper, since the installed capacity follows from the slope of the SF, and daily means reduce the effect of building thermal inertia \cite{Per17b}.}
% \textcolor{blue}{Since $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\max}$ is assumed to be known for  }
% , or a proxy such as the summed peak of the individual TCLs.

An aggregate may have a mix of TCLs with different behaviours with respect to temperature. While some TCLs can be active in different regimes of the net-load fit, such as reversible HPs that can heat and cool, others are exclusive to either heating (e.g. resistive heating) or cooling (e.g. air-conditioning). Using the total TCL installed capacity in \eqref{eq:sf} would lead to an underestimation of the SF, since the installed capacity of cooling-exclusive devices would still count towards the SF in the heating regime, and \emph{vice-versa}. Therefore, each regime will have its own capacity, which is defined as
\begin{equation}
    P_k^{\max} = \sum_{n\in \mathcal{N}_k}P_n^{\max},
\label{eq:pk}
\end{equation}
where $k$ denotes the regime (heating or cooling), $\mathcal{N}_k$ is the set of TCLs active in regime $k$, and $P_n^{\max}$ is the installed capacity of TCL $n$. The true value of SF is then defined per heating and cooling regime as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{SF}_h(T) &= {P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)}/{P_h^{\max}}, \quad \forall  T < T_h,\\
    \mathrm{SF}_c(T) &= {P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)}/{P_c^{\max}}, \quad \forall T > T_c,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sf-piecewise}
\end{equation}

with $h$ denoting heating and $c$ cooling. It is by definition bounded in $[0,1]$. The SF fit takes the same form as \eqref{eq:bathtub},
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \hat{\mathrm{SF}}_h(T) &= b_h + m_h (T_h - T), \quad \forall T < T_h,\\
    \hat{\mathrm{SF}}_c(T) &= b_c + m_c ( T - T_c), \quad \forall T > T_c,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sffit}
\end{equation}
where $T_h$ and $T_c$ are the thresholds of the net-load fit \eqref{eq:bathtub} of the same aggregate. The SF fit does not fit thresholds of its own. It borrows them from the net-load fit, so both fits describe the same heating and cooling regimes. The intercepts $b_h$ and $b_c$ and the heating and cooling SF sensitivities $m_h$ and $m_c$ [1/$^\circ$C] are determined by OLS on the days of each regime. The intercepts capture the weather-independent share of TCL use, such as domestic hot water, which the net-load fit instead places in $P_{\mathrm{base}}$. Fig.~\ref{fig:sf} shows the SF data points and the SF fit for the same Austin population of Fig.~\ref{fig:bathtub}.

\caption{SF fit \eqref{eq:sffit} to the aggregated Austin TCLs \cite{pecanstreet}, with each daily point normalised by the installed capacity of the TCLs active in its regime \eqref{eq:sf-piecewise}.}
\label{fig:sf}
\end{figure}

The observed SF stays well below 1. Each arm would reach the installed capacity of its regime, with every TCL drawing at once, only at temperatures well outside any observed weather. The gap between the observed TCL load and the installed capacity, the unused share $1-\mathrm{SF}$ of that capacity, is what the flexibility of Section~\ref{sec:flex} draws on.

R1, R3, or both are resolved as the local climate dictates, and each is scored on the days of its own regime. R1 fits with a net-load $R^2_h$ between 0.68 and 0.92 across the year-round aggregates, lowest in Austin where electric heating is marginal. R3 ranges from $R^2_c$ of 0.95 in hot Maricopa to 0.75 in mild Oregon, where cooling is rare.

The threshold temperatures follow the local climate. $T_h$ falls between 8 and 17\,$^\circ$C, and $T_c$ between 18 and 26\,$^\circ$C. A hot county such as Maricopa has a steep R3, whereas a cold one such as Hamelin has none. The sensitivities $s_h$ and $s_c$ of the net-load fit scale with the aggregate's total installed TCL, and therefore with its size. The 849-household Norwegian aggregate and the electrified ResStock counties show the steepest arms for this reason. The SF sensitivities $m_h$ and $m_c$ remove this size dependence.

The SF sensitivities $m_h$ and $m_c$ group partly by K{\"o}ppen class. The two temperate oceanic (Cfb) aggregates, Hamelin and Kloten, sit at $m_h=0.016$ and $0.018\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$, despite running on water-source and mixed ground-and-air-source heat pumps respectively. The two humid continental (Dfa and Dfb) sites, Oslo and Hennepin, converge on $m_h\approx0.022\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$, even though Oslo's heating load is mostly ground-source and direct resistance, while Hennepin's is air-source. The temperate dry and warm summer (Csb) class, however, splits. Its two measured aggregates, the Washington and Oregon households with ductless HPs, sit at $m_h=0.023$--$0.024\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$, whereas the simulated King County aggregate of ducted HPs with resistance backup reaches $0.036\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$. The cooling sensitivity $m_c$ spans $0.024$--$0.038\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$ without a clear grouping. The hot desert site, Maricopa, is the outlier, with the steepest heating sensitivity of all, $m_h=0.063\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$. This is possibly because a single aggregate and few genuinely cold days leave little data to anchor the coldest end of its heating arm. Overall, the K{\"o}ppen class narrows $m_h$ for aggregates of similar TCL technology, but the technology, and possibly the difference between measured and simulated peaks, matters as well. Across classes, $m_h$ varies by up to a factor of four, so the transfer of Section~\ref{sec:transfer} is only expected to hold within a climate class and TCL technology. \chg{Section~\ref{sec:transfer-pop} tests this expectation on further populations with complete heating submetering.}

\subsection{Transferability of SF}
\label{sec:transfer}

The SF is computed from submetered TCL data, which a DSO rarely holds for the aggregate under study. The SF fit \eqref{eq:sffit} is therefore calibrated on a submetered pilot and reused on aggregates of the same population that have no submetering. Throughout, a population is the set of households of one dataset or study; its pilot is the submetered part used for calibration, a target is an aggregate the estimate is applied to, and a split is one random partition of a population into a pilot and targets. Only the SF sensitivities $m_h$ and $m_c$ transfer. The pilot's thresholds come from the pilot's own net-load fit and follow its building stock and climate, so the target's thresholds $T_h$ and $T_c$ are read from the target's net-load fit instead. The pilot's intercepts $b_h$ and $b_c$ describe weather-independent TCL use, which the target's net-load fit places in $P_{\mathrm{base}}$ and never attributes to its TCL arms.

The transfer rests on one hypothesis: within a population, $m_h$ and $m_c$ do not track the installed capacity or the TCL penetration of the aggregate. Section~\ref{sec:res-sf} tests this hypothesis within the Kloten population, and Section~\ref{sec:validation} tests it across populations. If it holds, the transferred sensitivity turns the net-load fit into an estimate of the unobserved installed capacity, through \eqref{eq:capacity}. This is the mechanism the rest of the paper builds on.

However, this is out of scope and not considered for the datasets used in the rest of this paper.}

\section{Aggregated TCL Quantification}
\label{sec:estimation}

The two fits, \eqref{eq:tclfit} and \eqref{eq:sffit}, are complementary. The net-load fit \eqref{eq:tclfit} needs no submetering, but its sensitivities also absorb any temperature response of the non-TCL load. The SF fit \eqref{eq:sffit} needs submetering but isolates the TCL simultaneity. Together they estimate three quantities a DSO needs about the TCLs of an aggregate: their energy consumption, installed capacity and flexibility potential. Energy consumption follows from \eqref{eq:tclfit} alone. Installed capacity and flexibility also need the transferred SF sensitivity, since the net load exposes the aggregated TCL demand, not the non-coincident sum of the individual peaks. Section~\ref{sec:results} evaluates each quantity.

\subsection{TCL Energy Consumption Estimation}
\label{sec:uc-energy}

The energy the TCL population draws follows from integrating its temperature response over a certain period.
%In the heating regime, $T < T_h$, the fitted TCL load of Equation~\eqref{eq:tclfit} reduces to its heating arm $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T) = s_h (T_h - T)$.
Over a day of mean temperature $T$, the temperature-driven TCL energy is given by
\begin{equation}
\hat{E}_{\mathrm{TCL}} = \left ( s_h \max(0, T_h - T) + s_c \max(0, T-T_c) \right ) \,\Delta t,
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta t$ being the length of the period, here one day. The annual energy is the sum of \eqref{eq:energy} over the days of the year. The fitted TCL load alone suffices. Given a measured temperature, it recovers the aggregated TCL load $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)$ from the net load $P_{\mathrm{net}}(t)$; given a forecast temperature, it forecasts that load for a future period.

R2 bounds the estimate. Between $T_h$ and $T_c$ both arms are zero. The estimate therefore misses any TCL energy that persists into mild weather, such as domestic hot water. The estimate captures the temperature-driven share, not the annual total.

\subsection{TCL Installed Capacity Estimation}
\label{sec:uc-capacity}

The installed capacity is the sum of the individual peak demands \eqref{eq:pk}, defined per regime. The aggregated TCLs never draw it at once, so the net load never exposes it directly. The net-load fit does expose the temperature-driven TCL load $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\,i}(T)$ of aggregate $i$. Rearranging \eqref{eq:sf-piecewise}, the capacity of regime $k$ follows from this load and the SF at the same temperature.

Both must describe the same load. The net-load arm is zero at the aggregate's own threshold by construction, and the weather-independent TCL use sits in $P_{\mathrm{base}}$. The matching SF is therefore the temperature-driven part of \eqref{eq:sffit}. It starts from zero at the aggregate's own thresholds and grows with the transferred sensitivity,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \hat{\mathrm{SF}}_h^{\,i}(T) &= m_h\,(T_h^{\,i} - T), \quad \forall T < T_h^{\,i},\\
    \hat{\mathrm{SF}}_c^{\,i}(T) &= m_c\,(T - T_c^{\,i}), \quad \forall T > T_c^{\,i}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sf-target}
\end{equation}
Dividing the arms of \eqref{eq:tclfit} by \eqref{eq:sf-target} gives the installed capacity of each regime,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \hat{P}_h^{\max,i} &= \frac{s_h^{\,i}\,(T_h^{\,i} - T)}{m_h\,(T_h^{\,i} - T)} = \frac{s_h^{\,i}}{m_h}, \quad \forall T < T_h^{\,i},\\
    \hat{P}_c^{\max,i} &= \frac{s_c^{\,i}\,(T - T_c^{\,i})}{m_c\,(T - T_c^{\,i})} = \frac{s_c^{\,i}}{m_c}, \quad \forall T > T_c^{\,i}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:capacity}
\end{equation}
The temperature cancels, so the estimate needs no choice of evaluation day. The net-load fit supplies the numerator, which the DSO observes. The pilot supplies only the denominator $m_k$. The estimate is therefore only as good as the transferred sensitivity, which Section~\ref{sec:results} tests.

The two regimes are estimated separately. Their sum equals the total installed TCL capacity only when no device serves both regimes. A reversible HP counts in both $\hat{P}_h^{\max}$ and $\hat{P}_c^{\max}$. For a population of reversible HPs, the total therefore lies between the larger of the two and their sum.

\subsection{Flexibility Quantification}
\label{sec:uc-flex}
\label{sec:flex}

The flexibility a TCL population offers is the energy it can shift from its baseline consumption without breaching indoor thermal comfort \cite{Zir19}. Building-stock insulation and indoor-comfort data are lacking, so an abstract time window $\Delta t_{Th}$ stands in. Within it, the population must deliver a fixed thermal energy to stay comfortable. A larger $\Delta t_{Th}$ means a better-insulated building, or wider comfort bands \cite{Cha16}.
Over this $\Delta t_{Th}$, the aggregated TCLs must deliver the temperature-set energy demand. This is the temperature-driven TCL energy of \eqref{eq:energy}, evaluated over the window $\Delta t_{Th}$ and drawn at the baseline power $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T) = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}$,
\begin{equation}
\hat{E}_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\Delta t_{Th}} = \hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)\,\Delta t_{Th} = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th},
\label{eq:flex-baseline}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}$ and $\hat{P}^{\max}$ are those of the active regime, from \eqref{eq:sf-target} and \eqref{eq:capacity}. Their product equals the fitted TCL load of \eqref{eq:tclfit}, so the baseline is the temperature-driven load of the net-load fit. The schedule that delivers this same energy while deviating most from the baseline is the two-level square wave of Fig.~\ref{fig:flex-schedule}: full installed power $\hat{P}^{\max}$ for a fraction $\alpha_F$ of the window and zero for the rest. Its energy $\alpha_F\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th}$ must equal \eqref{eq:flex-baseline}, which fixes the duty cycle at the simultaneity factor,
\begin{equation}
\alpha_F\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th} = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th} \;\Longrightarrow\; \alpha_F = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T).
\label{eq:flex-duty}
\end{equation}
The flexible energy is this schedule's symmetric deviation from the baseline. During the on phase the population draws $\hat{P}^{\max} - \hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}$ above baseline for a time $\alpha_F \Delta t_{Th}$; during the off phase it curtails $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}$ for a time $(1 - \alpha_F)\Delta t_{Th}$. The two areas are equal, giving an upward and downward flexible energy
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{flex}} = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\big(1 - \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\big)\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th}.
\label{eq:flex-energy}
\end{equation}
This use case draws on both fits and one external parameter. Since $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}$ equals the fitted TCL load, \eqref{eq:flex-energy} equals $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)\,\big(1-\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\big)\,\Delta t_{Th}$. The net-load fit sets the load that can be shifted, and the transferred sensitivity only the unused capacity above it, so the capacity estimate cancels. The window $\Delta t_{Th}$, over which the population can be reshaped, is a property of the building stock, not of the fits. The instantaneous unused capacity $(1 - \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T))\,\hat{P}^{\max}$ is the special case of the on phase drawn to full power.

Over a year, this paper counts one such window per day of R1 or R3, the regimes in which \eqref{eq:sf-target} is defined. The annual flexible energy is then the sum of \eqref{eq:flex-energy} over these days, each evaluated at its daily mean temperature, and it scales linearly with $\Delta t_{Th}$. The days of R2 are left out of the estimate and of its ground truth alike. The estimate therefore covers only temperature-driven flexibility; weather-independent use that could also be shifted, such as hot-water heating, falls outside it.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_flex_schedule.pdf}
\vspace{-10pt}
\caption{The flexibility of \eqref{eq:flex-energy} over one cycle of the square-wave schedule. The aggregate must draw the baseline energy $E^{\mathrm{old}}_{\mathrm{TCL}} = \hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)\,\Delta t_{Th}$ (hatched) over the window. It can instead draw the same energy as the full-power pulse $P^{\mathrm{new}}_{\mathrm{TCL}}$ of duty cycle $\alpha_F = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)$. The deviation from baseline is the flexible energy, equal upward ($E_{\mathrm{flex}}^{\uparrow}$) and downward ($E_{\mathrm{flex}}^{\downarrow}$).}
\label{fig:flex-schedule}
\end{figure}

The bound is an idealisation, not a dispatchable schedule. It takes the square wave as feasible and ignores each building's thermal state and the comfort limits within which its TCLs can shift. It therefore stands as a potential against temperature and window length, not as a firm reserve.

\section{Numerical Results}

\subsection{Kloten Data and Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:data}

This section uses a single Swiss population, the households metered at the Kloten weather station in calendar 2023. The data combines two sources, the HEAPO dataset \cite{Bru25} and an additional smart-meter dataset \cite{Kai26b}. The load profiles have 15-minute resolution, while the ambient temperature is hourly. The only TCL used throughout is the HP and only heating is observed. The station holds 50 households with a submetered HP and 1307 households with neither an HP nor any other electric heating.

The 50 HPs are split at random into two halves of 25, twenty times over. In each split, the first half is the submetered pilot: its aggregated SF is fitted with \eqref{eq:sffit} and supplies the sensitivity $m_h$. Semi-synthetic aggregates, i.e. sums of measured households, are then drawn from the second half only, so no HP informs both the pilot and a target. Each aggregate combines $N_{hp}$ distinct HPs of that half, with the same households' other load, and $N - N_{hp}$ distinct households without an HP. The consumer count $N$ takes the values 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50, and $N_{hp}$ the values 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25, with $N_{hp} \le N$. Every feasible cell holds three aggregates per split, giving 1260 aggregates. All draws are without replacement, so no household recurs within an aggregate.

Two error metrics recur below. The weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE) scores an estimate $\hat{y}_i$ against its ground truth $y_i$ across the aggregates $i$,
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{WAPE} = \frac{\sum_i |\hat{y}_i - y_i|}{\sum_i y_i} \times 100\%,
	\label{eq:wape}
\end{equation}
so WAPE weights each aggregate by its true magnitude, not equally. A small aggregate, with a large percentage error but a small absolute error, therefore cannot dominate. The metric reports the total error across a set of aggregates, as a DSO would read it. The median ratio, the median of $\hat{y}_i / y_i$, gives the direction of the error, $< 1$ for an underestimate and $> 1$ for an overestimate.

\subsection{Fit Quality across Spatial and Temporal Aggregation}
\label{sec:res-ff}

Both fits, \eqref{eq:bathtub} and \eqref{eq:sffit}, are made to each aggregate's measurements, varying the number of consumers and the resolution. The HP penetration is kept fixed at 50\%, on a separate set of Kloten aggregates, also drawn without replacement. Spatial aggregation averages out the intermittent, non-thermal behaviour, as more meters are summed behind one aggregate. Temporal aggregation does the same over time, averaging the load over longer windows removes the occupancy swings and building thermal inertia that do not track temperature one-to-one at finer resolutions. Fig.~\ref{fig:quality-heatmap} reports both, as the median $R^2_h$ of the net-load and SF fits over every consumer-count/averaging-window cell at this fixed HP penetration. Both fits are scored on the points below the net-load threshold $T_h$, at which the SF fit is anchored.

\subsection{Energy Consumption Estimation}
\label{sec:res-energy}

Equation~\eqref{eq:energy} needs only the net-load fit, so the energy is the one estimate that requires no pilot. On the days below the threshold, R1 is compared against the submetered HP energy of each aggregate. Across the 1260 aggregates, the estimate reaches a WAPE of 9\% at a median ratio of 0.97. \subsection{Installed Capacity Estimation}
\label{sec:res-capacity}

Equation~\eqref{eq:capacity} estimates the installed HP capacity of each aggregate as the ratio of two sensitivities. The numerator $s_h$ is read from the aggregate's own net-load fit. The denominator $m_h$ is the sensitivity of the pilot of the same split (Section~\ref{sec:res-sf}). The target aggregate is therefore never submetered.

With the transferred sensitivity, \eqref{eq:capacity} recovers the installed capacity of the 1260 aggregates at a WAPE of 19\%, with a median overestimate of 16\%. In absolute terms, the mean error is 17~kW, the capacity of about three HPs of the mean 6.3~kW. The overestimate is consistent with the non-TCL temperature response that the net-load sensitivity absorbs, the neglected term of \eqref{eq:decomp} discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. The error shrinks as HPs are added: the WAPE falls from 41\% at five HPs to 15\% at twenty-five, and the median overestimate from 35\% to 12\%. This is possibly because the households without an HP, whose temperature response inflates the heating arm, weigh less against more HPs.

\subsection{Flexibility Potential}
\label{sec:res-flex}

The capacity estimate enters \eqref{eq:flex-energy} only in appearance. Since $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}$ equals the fitted TCL load, the estimate reduces to $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)\,\big(1-\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\big)\,\Delta t_{Th}$: the temperature-driven load of the net-load fit, times the unused share of capacity, $1-\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)$, left by the transferred SF. The actual flexibility is computed at hourly resolution: each hour's submetered SF gives $\mathrm{SF}(1-\mathrm{SF})\,P_h^{\max}$ with the submetered capacity, averaged over the day. Both are summed over the days of R1, below the aggregate's net-load threshold, since the Kloten aggregates have no cooling regime. The estimate and its ground truth share no input other than the HP load contained in the net load, so their agreement does not follow by construction.

Fig.~\ref{fig:flex-ratio} gives the distribution of the estimated-to-actual ratio of annual flexible energy across the 1260 aggregates. The ratio has a median of 1.04 and an interquartile range of [0.97, 1.17], at a WAPE of 10\%. It splits into two factors. The R1 energy of Section~\ref{sec:res-energy} contributes a median ratio of 0.97, and the unused capacity a median ratio of 1.07. About three points of this unused-capacity factor come from the transferred SF running slightly below the measured one, and about four from the daily resolution of the estimate, since the SF swings within the day. The flexibility and energy ratios correlate at 0.96. The flexibility estimate therefore inherits its accuracy mainly from the net-load fit, while the transferred sensitivity shifts the unused capacity by a few per cent. Since $\Delta t_{Th}$ cancels in the ratio, neither factor depends on the window.

Computed from the daily mean SF instead, as the estimate is, the actual flexibility would be 3\% higher and the median ratio 1.01, since $\mathrm{SF}(1-\mathrm{SF})$ is concave. The days of R2, left out by construction, would add a weather-independent floor, most plausibly hot-water heating, worth a median 9\% of the actual flexible energy over the whole year. On the coldest day, the estimate recovers the flexible energy at a WAPE of 21\% and an $R^2$ of 0.73 against the actual values across aggregates.

\section{Real Aggregates}
\label{sec:validation}

\subsection{\chg{Real Aggregates outside the Pilot}}
\label{sec:real-agg}

Section~\ref{sec:results} tested the method within one population, on aggregates built from its households. This section applies it to real aggregates that no pilot has seen, each summed from its own field meters. Swiss and German aggregates test the heating regime with the Kloten pilot. Submetered data serve only to validate the results, never as input.

Three real aggregates lie outside the Kloten pilot. Two are the HEAPO households of two further Swiss weather stations, Hg and MqO, with 21 and 6 submetered HPs. The third comes from the WPuQ measurement campaign in Hamelin \cite{Sch22}: its net load is the sum of 37 individually metered German single-family houses, each with a submetered HP. All three are summed rather than metered at a transformer, so they carry no network losses. They also contain no households without an HP, which makes their HP penetration 100\%. The pilot now comprises all 50 Kloten HPs. Its SF fit, at the pilot's own net-load threshold, gives $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}_h(T) = 0.029 + 0.018\,(16.6 - T)$, and only its sensitivity $m_h = 0.018\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$ transfers.

Table~\ref{tab:real} reports the three estimates. The capacity errors are $+2\%$ at Hg, $-6\%$ at MqO and $-9\%$ at Hamelin. MqO holds only six HPs, below the ten from which Section~\ref{sec:res-capacity} finds the estimates reliable, so its result is indicative. The flexibility ratio on the R1 days lies between 0.93 and 0.96, slightly below the Kloten median of 1.04 (Section~\ref{sec:res-flex}). It follows the R1 energy ratios of 0.89 to 0.92, while the unused-capacity factor stays between 1.01 and 1.05. The energy ratios are lower than on Kloten possibly because these aggregates hold no households without an HP, whose temperature response inflates the heating arm.

The net-load thresholds are $16.7\,^\circ$C at Hg, $14.0\,^\circ$C at MqO and $13.9\,^\circ$C at Hamelin, against $16.6\,^\circ$C for the Kloten pilot. The threshold temperature varies with the insulation of the building stock, the heat released indoors by occupants and appliances, and the preferred indoor setpoint \cite{Men18}. Transferring the full SF curve from Kloten, intercept and threshold included, gives capacity errors of $-3\%$, $-20\%$ and $-26\%$. The error of the full curve therefore grows with the distance between the target's threshold and the pilot's, whereas the sensitivity alone stays within 10\%. \chg{Unlike within Kloten, the full curve loses to the sensitivity alone here, since the targets' thresholds lie up to $2.7\,^\circ$C below the pilot's. The planning estimate of Section~\ref{sec:res-capacity} errs by $+4\%$, $-6\%$ and $-26\%$: at Hamelin the span between threshold and coldest day is only $19\,^\circ$C, against $25\,^\circ$C at Kloten, so the coldest-day SF falls well below 0.425. With each target's own SF sensitivity, the errors are $+6\%$, $+5\%$ and $+3\%$. This error of the net-load sensitivity remains small since every household in these aggregates has an HP.}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Accuracy}

The capacity estimate carries two error sources. The first is the non-TCL temperature response that \eqref{eq:decomp} neglects, which biases the net-load numerator upwards. A zero-HP control makes this concrete. Aggregates of 10 to 50 Kloten households without an HP still show a small heating arm, from which \eqref{eq:capacity} infers a median 0.28~kW of spurious capacity per household. This floor is about 4\% of the mean installed capacity of one HP, 6.3~kW. It therefore adds about 45\% to the estimate at 10\% HP penetration, but only about 4\% once every consumer has an HP.

The second source is the transferred sensitivity. Within the Kloten population, the pilot's sensitivity matches the aggregates' own at a median ratio of 1.00, with an interquartile range of $\pm10\%$, so the transfer adds spread rather than bias. \chg{Table~\ref{tab:baselines} sizes the two sources on the Kloten aggregates: with each aggregate's own SF sensitivity, the estimate errs at a WAPE of 14.5\%, and with the pilot's at 19.3\%. On the real aggregates outside the pilot, where every household has an HP,} the estimates lie within 10\% of the submetered capacity.

\subsection{Generalisation}

The SF generalises in a specific and testable way. Within a population, its sensitivity is empirically stable against the number of HPs and their installed capacity over the tested range, so a curve from a submetered pilot serves that population's aggregates without submetering. Across populations, only the sensitivity transfers. The threshold temperature follows the local building stock and climate, and the net-load fit supplies it from data the DSO already meters. The intercept describes weather-independent TCL use, which lies outside the temperature-driven quantities estimated here. Transferring the full curve instead of the sensitivity alone raises the capacity error to 20\% at MqO and 26\% at Hamelin.

\chg{The sensitivity itself transfers between populations that share a climate zone and a housing stock. Between random splits of one population, between studies of one climate zone and between countries of one climate class, it stays within 12\% of the target's own (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}). Between heating zones and between social and private housing, however, it differs by factors of 1.7 and 1.65, more than between HP technologies. Across the climate classes of Table~\ref{tab:cross}, it varies by up to a factor of four. Generalisation therefore reduces to transferring a single parameter from a pilot of the same climate zone, TCL technology and housing stock. This mirrors the inverse problem solved for PV installed capacity against irradiance \cite{gouveia2026pvcapacity}, here posed against ambient temperature.}

\subsection{Data Requirements}

The two fits ask for different data. The net-load fit needs only the aggregated net load and ambient temperature, both already held by the DSO, and runs on any aggregate. The SF fit needs submetered TCL load and installed capacity, which the DSO rarely holds. A single submetered pilot of the same population calibrates it, and its sensitivity then transfers, so no aggregate under study is submetered. A pilot of a few tens of HPs appears to suffice: a pilot of 25 Kloten HPs reproduces the SF shape of the other 25 at a median $R^2_h$ of 0.91, and recovers their capacity at a median error of $+8\%$ (Section~\ref{sec:res-sf}). The pilot should, however, submeter every electric heating source of its households, including backup resistance heat.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limits temper the results. The within-population tests rest on the 50 submetered Kloten HPs. The semi-synthetic aggregates are drawn without replacement and never share an HP with their pilot, but aggregates of the same split draw on the same 25 HPs and therefore overlap. The cross-population tests of the full estimator rest on three real aggregates in the heating regime, one of them with only six HPs, \chg{and all with an HP in every household. Table~\ref{tab:transfer} tests the SF transfer alone on further populations, but not the net-load numerator.} The cooling regime remains untested. \chg{The only aggregates with complete cooling ground truth, the NEEA households of Washington and Oregon \cite{neea_eulr}, allow no cooling transfer, since the Washington pilot has no day above its cooling threshold in 2023. The Oregon aggregate also shows that the numerator can fail on its own: even with its own SF sensitivities, its capacity is overestimated by 45\% in heating and 72\% in cooling. About a third of its net-load heating sensitivity lies outside the metered heating circuits. The cooling fits of Table~\ref{tab:cross} are therefore descriptive only.} The model neglects the non-TCL temperature response throughout, which the bias at a low HP count exposes.

A further confounder is deliberate control. Swiss and German domestic HPs commonly run under interruptible tariffs, under which the utility may cut the HP supply during defined blocking periods, and since January 2024 {\S}14a EnWG lets German DSOs throttle HPs above 4.2~kW \cite{Bna23}. Such control truncates the aggregated TCL peak. It may bias the net-load numerator and the capacity estimate downwards, and imprints regime-specific structure that a single-sensitivity transfer need not capture. On the real aggregates, however, the estimates lie within 10\% of the submetered capacity, so such control is not a visible source of error there. The WPuQ campaign predates {\S}14a, but blocking-period tariffs do not. Underlying all of these is the broader shortage of submetered TCL data, which a wider pilot would relieve.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented a method to estimate the installed capacity of the TCLs behind a feeder from its net load and ambient temperature. Below a balance-point temperature, the daily net load and the SF both rise linearly as the temperature falls. The ratio of the two slopes gives the installed capacity, and only the SF slope is calibrated on a small set of submetered households. On 1260 aggregates built from measured Swiss households, the capacity was recovered at a WAPE of 19\%, falling to 12--14\% for aggregates of fifty households at 50\% HP penetration. Most of this error stems from the net-load slope, which also includes the temperature response of households without an HP, rather than from the transfer of the SF slope. On three real Swiss and German aggregates where every household has an HP, the estimates came within 10\% of the submetered capacity, whereas transferring the full calibrated SF curve erred by up to 26\%. Across submetered households in five countries, the SF slope transferred within one climate zone but not between climate zones or between social and private housing. The calibration households should therefore match the target in climate, TCL technology and housing stock. The same fits give the temperature-driven energy without any submetered households, at a WAPE of 9\%.

The method thus trades a modest, characterised error for a large gain in deployability. Its estimates are least reliable on aggregates with a handful of HPs and most reliable once tens of HPs are aggregated, which is the regime a substation presents. Where no submetered households are available, the net-load fit alone still gives the temperature-driven energy.

Regarding future work, four directions follow. The submetered populations were small, so a wider and more diverse set of metered HPs, with every electric heating source submetered, would test the transfer on more populations and in the cooling regime. The net-load sensitivity also absorbs the temperature response of the non-TCL load, which inflates the capacity estimate most where consumers without TCLs dominate. Future work will compensate the estimates for this response, for instance by subtracting a per-consumer non-TCL sensitivity scaled by the consumer count the DSO already holds. It will also evaluate data-driven methods for HP and TCL quantification against the proposed estimator, to see where a learned model earns its data cost and where the proposed estimator suffices. Finally, it will model the TCL consumption that does not depend on temperature, chiefly domestic hot water and baseline operation. The temperature-driven estimate omits this consumption, which sets the gap between the temperature-driven and the annual energy.
