\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The electrification of residential heating and cooling is a central lever of decarbonisation policy. Its devices are thermostatically controlled loads (TCLs), such as heat pumps (HPs), electric resistance heaters and air conditioners, and the HP leads their uptake \cite{Iea22}. In 2025, about 2.9 million HPs were sold in 21 European countries, 13\% more than in 2024, which brought the installed stock to 29.3 million \cite{Ehp26}. Feeder data show the same trend, with installed HP power on 200 German low-voltage (LV) feeders up by over twenty percent from 2023 to 2025 \cite{Tre26}.

The energy demand of these devices follows the outdoor temperature, and their uptake loads LV networks that may not have been sized for this load. Field and simulation studies show that TCLs raise the coincident peak of feeders and transformers, i.e.\ the highest load their devices draw together, in extreme weather. HPs are the most studied case. Their peak is sharpest in cold spells, when many devices run at once \cite{Vel11, Akm14, Som21, Bec24, Lia26}. Electric vehicles (EVs) add further load to the same feeders \cite{Bec24}.

A DSO needs the installed capacity of the TCLs behind each feeder, rather than only their coincident peak, for three reasons. First, the installed capacity scales with adoption, so it supports planning scenarios as the number of TCLs grows. Second, the installed capacity bounds the peak the TCLs can draw when they are synchronised. Third, network planning sizes transformers and cables for a design peak. This design peak is the installed capacity multiplied by the simultaneity factor (SF), the share of the installed capacity drawn at once \cite{Few24}. For HPs, it is set by cold weather, when their coincident demand peaks \cite{Lov17, Che21b, Rou23}. The SF also bounds the flexibility the TCLs can offer \cite{Mul19, Zir19, Dhu15}, since only the capacity not in use can be switched on.

A DSO rarely knows this capacity. Privacy concerns and limits on metering and data exchange restrict the DSO's access to household data, and its records of distributed energy resources (DERs) are often incomplete or out of date \cite{Gou26}. What the DSO does hold is the net load metered at the substation or feeder head, and the outdoor temperature. Where smart meters are rolled out, the DSO also meters each household, but its access to these data is often restricted. The DSO usually knows how many households each feeder serves \cite{Tre26}. The installed capacity of the TCLs behind a feeder therefore stays largely unobserved.

\input{tables/tab_litreview}

Five lines of research address this unobserved capacity, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:litreview}. Non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM) and HP detection recover thermostatic load from customer meters, either as a present-absent label \cite{Fei13, Wei20b, Ray19} or as a disaggregated energy time series \cite{Bru23, Gis26}. Some of these methods extend to LV substations \cite{Wan20, Oma24}. They need labelled or submetered training data, however, and stop short of the installed capacity. At the substation, they have been validated mostly on synthetic substation aggregates rather than on measured substation data.

Change-point and degree-day models fit load as a piecewise-linear function of temperature. They range from canonical building-level formulations \cite{Fel08, Ruc92, Kis04}, reviewed in \cite{Faz16}, to aggregated and smart-meter populations \cite{Ali11, Per17b, Mac19}. These models recover heating and cooling sensitivities and a threshold temperature without any labels. They stop, however, at the coincident response and never reach the installed capacity, the sum of the individual peak demands.

A separate body of work models aggregated TCL populations for control. A population can be described through the thermal parameters of its devices, either to deliver load following 
% this sentence is weird
\cite{Cal09} or as a stochastic battery whose power and energy limits bound its flexibility \cite{Hao15}. The state of such a population can be estimated from its aggregate power \cite{Mat13}, and the air-conditioning demand of a feeder can be disaggregated in real time from feeder measurements \cite{Led18}. These methods assume known device models, or learn them from building- and device-level data. They also work at control time scales, and none of them estimates how much TCL capacity is installed behind the meter.

For photovoltaics (PV) and other DERs, by contrast, installed-capacity estimation is well developed. Methods infer the capacity from aggregated net load or smart-meter data \cite{Was21, Liu22, Gou25, gouveia2026pvcapacity}. Irradiance plays the role there that temperature could play for TCLs, yet the TCL counterpart is largely missing \cite{Gou26}.

Studies of HP demand measure the coincident peak, as an after-diversity maximum demand, or model the coincidence factor, on submetered populations of known size \cite{Lov17, Che21b, Rou23}. Closest to this paper, Anderson et al.\ \cite{And20} fit a probabilistic model of HP demand against temperature to submetered customer data. They use this model to assess LV networks under a given HP penetration. Such models run in the forward direction, from a known number of HPs to the demand they cause. None of them recovers the installed capacity of a feeder whose households are not submetered.

To the best of the authors' knowledge, no prior method estimates the installed capacity of an aggregated TCL population from feeder-level net load without submetering the households of the target feeder. Change-point models observe the right signal, the rise of the net load as the temperature falls, but stop at the coincident response. Simultaneity studies make the step from coincident demand to installed capacity, but only on submetered populations. TCL control models, in turn, assume that the population is already known. 

This paper joins the change-point and simultaneity approaches. It estimates the installed capacity from the net load and temperature of a feeder, together with the SF sensitivity, i.e.\ how fast the SF grows as the temperature falls. The SF sensitivity is calibrated once on a small pilot. The pilot is a set of households whose heating is submetered, i.e.\ metered separately from the rest of their load, so that their installed heating capacity can be measured. On the feeder under study, called the target, the net load also grows as the temperature falls, at a rate the DSO reads from its own data. This rate, the net-load sensitivity, is larger the more TCL capacity is installed behind the feeder. Dividing the net-load sensitivity of the target by the SF sensitivity of the pilot therefore gives the installed capacity of the target. The households of the target are never submetered, so one pilot serves many feeders.

The contributions of this paper are threefold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A method to estimate the installed TCL capacity behind a feeder from its net load and temperature, calibrated once on a small submetered pilot. The households of the target feeder are never submetered.
\item An evaluation of when the SF sensitivity of a pilot transfers: within one population, across HP penetration, aggregate size and data resolution; to real aggregates outside the pilot and to populations from five countries.
\item Estimates of the temperature-driven TCL energy, which needs only the net-load sensitivity and no pilot, and of a flexibility potential, which also uses the SF sensitivity.
\end{enumerate}

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the problem, defines two fits of the temperature response, one to the net load and one to the pilot's SF, and characterises them across datasets. Section~\ref{sec:estimation} derives the installed capacity, energy and flexibility from these fits. Section~\ref{sec:results} evaluates them on semi-synthetic aggregates of one Swiss population, and Section~\ref{sec:validation} applies the method to real Swiss and German aggregates outside the pilot. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses accuracy, generalisation and data requirements. Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} maps each step of the method to the section that defines it. Lastly, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Problem and Approach}
\label{sec:problem}
\label{sec:fits}

For the TCLs behind a metered point of an LV network, this paper estimates three quantities: their installed capacity, their temperature-driven energy and their flexibility potential. A metered point, such as a substation or feeder, together with the households behind it, is called an aggregate. The energy needs only the net load of the aggregate and the outdoor temperature, both held by the DSO. The installed capacity and the flexibility also need the SF sensitivity, calibrated once on a submetered pilot (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}).

The net load is the sum of a TCL and a non-TCL component,
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{net}}(t) = P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(t) + P_{\text{non-TCL}}(t),
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
where $t$ denotes time and only the TCL component responds directly to temperature. The non-TCL component also carries a weaker, indirect temperature dependence, visible in the whole-home energy signature \cite{Wes20}. Still, TCLs dominate the total temperature response, so the model neglects the non-TCL contribution. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} revisits this assumption.

Two fits describe how the TCLs of an aggregate respond to the outdoor temperature. The first, the net-load fit, uses only the net load and temperature the DSO holds (Section~\ref{sec:fits-net}). The second, the SF fit, needs the submetered TCL load and installed capacity, which in practice only a pilot provides (Section~\ref{sec:fits-sf}). Both fits share the same piecewise-linear form and use daily means, which reduce the effect of building thermal inertia \cite{Per17b}.

\subsection{Net-Load Fit}
\label{sec:fits-net}


When an aggregate's net load is averaged over each day and plotted against the daily mean temperature, it follows a piecewise-linear pattern. The load is roughly flat on mild days, and rises close to linearly as the temperature falls below, or rises above, a threshold.
%(ADD the table here we discussed)
 The net-load fit expresses the aggregated net load of \eqref{eq:decomp} as
\begin{equation}
\hat{P}_{\mathrm{net}}(T) = P_{\mathrm{base}} + s_h \max(0, T_h - T) + s_c \max(0, T - T_c),
\label{eq:bathtub}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the daily mean temperature [$^\circ$C], $P_{\mathrm{base}}$ the temperature-independent load [kW] and $s_h$ and $s_c$ the heating and cooling sensitivities [kW/$^\circ$C]. The heating and cooling threshold temperatures $T_h \le T_c$ [$^\circ$C], also known as balance-point temperatures, bound a dead band over which no thermal response is present. The parameters of the fitted curve are computed through Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), with $T_h$ and $T_c$ chosen as the values that minimise the squared residuals of the entire fit. The two temperature-dependent segments, called the heating and cooling arms of the fit, form the fitted aggregated TCL load.

%\textcolor{red}{should mention that $P_{\mathrm{base}}, s_h, s_c$ are the outcome of least square fit}

Fig.~\ref{fig:bathtub} shows the net-load fit to aggregated Austin smart-meter data \cite{pecanstreet}, with the parameters of \eqref{eq:bathtub} marked. The thresholds divide the temperature axis into three regimes, marked in Fig.~\ref{fig:bathtub}: the heating regime below $T_h$, the cooling regime above $T_c$ and the dead band between them, where the population neither heats nor cools. This work characterises the heating and cooling regimes alike and leaves the dead band out of scope. The evaluation of Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:validation} covers the heating regime only, since the available data allow no test of the transfer in the cooling regime (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\label{eq:tclfit}
\end{equation}

The fitted parameters carry a physical interpretation. The threshold temperatures $T_h$ and $T_c$ are behavioural. They mark where the population, on average, begins to heat or cool, rather than being a property of any building or device. Individual households start heating or cooling at different temperatures. Once many are aggregated, these differences average out, and $T_h$ and $T_c$ settle at values that reflect regional preferences for starting space heating or cooling (Section~\ref{sec:cross}).

The sensitivities $s_h$ and $s_c$ of the net-load fit measure how much heating or cooling load the population adds per degree.
% A steeper arm signals more installed TCLs.
They scale with the TCL capacity installed behind the meter, so an aggregate with more TCLs shows a steeper arm, all else being equal. Between aggregates of the same size, a steeper arm therefore also indicates a higher TCL penetration. Section~\ref{sec:res-sf} confirms this, with $s_h$ tracking installed capacity across the aggregates.

The balance between $s_h$ and $s_c$ reflects the local climate. Hot regions are expected to be cooling-dominated, with $s_h \approx 0$, whereas regions with long winters, such as the Nordic countries, are heating-dominated, with $s_c \approx 0$.



\subsection{Simultaneity-Factor Fit}
\label{sec:fits-sf}

% \textcolor{red}{
% What is simultaneity factor?
% why is it important?
% }


The SF is the ratio of the demand of a group of loads, here TCLs, at a given instant to the sum of their individual maximum demands. It reflects that connected devices do not all draw their full power at the same instant \cite{fani2024impact}. In distribution networks, the SF is central to planning. It lets utilities size transformers, cables and protection for the peak the group actually draws, rather than for the sum of individual peaks.


% The second function characterises the synchronous behaviour, or simultaneity, of the TCL population.
Mathematically, the SF is the share of the aggregate's installed capacity drawn at a given temperature $T$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SF}(T) = \frac{P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)}{P_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\max}},
\label{eq:sf}
\end{equation}
where $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)$ is the daily mean TCL load on a day of mean temperature $T$, and $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\max}$ is the total installed capacity of the TCLs in the aggregate. Nameplate ratings are not available for most households, so the installed capacity of each TCL is taken as the peak of its submetered 15-min draw. The installed capacity of an aggregate is thus the summed peak draw of its TCLs, which tracks their rated power.

\chg{Since $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)$ is a daily mean, the SF of this paper is a daily-mean SF. It is lower than the SF used in network planning, which takes the 15-min interval of highest aggregate demand; Section~\ref{sec:res-sf} gives the ratio between the two. The daily mean suits the aim of this paper, since the installed capacity follows from the SF sensitivity, and daily means reduce the effect of building thermal inertia \cite{Per17b}.}
% \textcolor{blue}{Since $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\max}$ is assumed to be known for  }
% , or a proxy such as the summed peak of the individual TCLs.

An aggregate may have a mix of TCLs with different behaviours with respect to temperature. Some TCLs, such as reversible HPs, can both heat and cool. Others only heat, such as resistive heaters, or only cool, such as air conditioners. Using the total TCL installed capacity in \eqref{eq:sf} would lead to an underestimation of the SF, since the installed capacity of cooling-exclusive devices would still count towards the SF in the heating regime, and \emph{vice-versa}. Therefore, each regime will have its own capacity, which is defined as
\begin{equation}
    P_k^{\max} = \sum_{n\in \mathcal{N}_k}P_n^{\max},
\label{eq:pk}
\end{equation}
where $k$ denotes the regime (heating or cooling), $\mathcal{N}_k$ is the set of TCLs active in regime $k$, and $P_n^{\max}$ is the installed capacity of TCL $n$. The measured SF is then defined per regime as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{SF}_h(T) &= {P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)}/{P_h^{\max}}, \quad \forall  T < T_h,\\
    \mathrm{SF}_c(T) &= {P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)}/{P_c^{\max}}, \quad \forall T > T_c,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sf-piecewise}
\end{equation}

with $h$ denoting heating and $c$ cooling. The SF is by definition bounded in $[0,1]$. The SF fit takes the same form as \eqref{eq:bathtub},
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \hat{\mathrm{SF}}_h(T) &= b_h + m_h (T_h - T), \quad \forall T < T_h,\\
    \hat{\mathrm{SF}}_c(T) &= b_c + m_c ( T - T_c), \quad \forall T > T_c,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sffit}
\end{equation}
where $T_h$ and $T_c$ are the thresholds of the net-load fit \eqref{eq:bathtub} of the same aggregate. The SF fit does not fit thresholds of its own. It borrows them from the net-load fit, so both fits describe the same heating and cooling regimes. The intercepts $b_h$ and $b_c$ and the heating and cooling SF sensitivities $m_h$ and $m_c$ [1/$^\circ$C] are determined by OLS on the days of each regime. The intercepts capture the weather-independent share of TCL use, such as domestic hot water, which the net-load fit instead places in $P_{\mathrm{base}}$. The SF fit therefore needs three inputs for the same aggregate: its daily submetered TCL load, its installed TCL capacity per regime and the daily mean temperature. Its thresholds come from the aggregate's own net-load fit. In practice, only a submetered pilot provides the first two (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). Fig.~\ref{fig:sf} shows the SF data points and the SF fit for the same Austin population of Fig.~\ref{fig:bathtub}.

\caption{SF fit \eqref{eq:sffit} to the aggregated Austin TCLs \cite{pecanstreet}, with each daily point normalised by the installed capacity of the TCLs active in its regime \eqref{eq:sf-piecewise}.}

The SF fit gives the SF at any temperature of its regime, which links it to the design peak of Section~\ref{sec:intro}. At a design temperature $T_{\mathrm{d}} < T_h$, the design SF is $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}_h(T_{\mathrm{d}}) = b_h + m_h (T_h - T_{\mathrm{d}})$, and the design peak is the installed capacity multiplied by this SF. Since the SF of this paper is a daily mean, this product is a daily-mean design load. The 15-min design peak lies above it by the peak-to-mean ratio of Section~\ref{sec:res-sf}.


%\textcolor{red}{where do you define how to calculate $m_h, m_c$}

The observed SF stays well below 1. Each arm would reach the installed capacity of its regime, with every TCL drawing at once, only at temperatures well outside any observed weather. The gap between the observed TCL load and the installed capacity, the unused share $1-\mathrm{SF}$ of that capacity, is what the flexibility of Section~\ref{sec:flex} draws on.

\subsection{Cross-dataset characterisation}
\label{sec:cross}

\input{tables/tab_cross}

Both fits hold across climates. Table~\ref{tab:cross} reports them on aggregates from four countries, with each climate given as its K{\"o}ppen--Geiger class \cite{beck2018koppen}. The heating regime, the cooling regime or both are resolved as the local climate dictates (Fig.~\ref{fig:bathtub}). Each fit is scored by its coefficient of determination on the days of its own regime, $R^2_h$ for heating and $R^2_c$ for cooling. In the heating regime, the net-load fit reaches an $R^2_h$ between 0.68 and 0.92 across the year-round aggregates. $R^2_h$ is lowest in Austin, where electric heating is marginal. In the cooling regime, $R^2_c$ ranges from 0.95 in hot Maricopa to 0.75 in mild Oregon, where cooling is rare.

The threshold temperatures follow the local climate. $T_h$ falls between 8 and 17\,$^\circ$C, and $T_c$ between 18 and 26\,$^\circ$C. A hot county such as Maricopa has a steep cooling arm, whereas a cold one such as Hamelin has no cooling whatsoever. The sensitivities $s_h$ and $s_c$ of the net-load fit scale with the aggregate's total installed TCL, and therefore with its size. The 849-household Norwegian aggregate and the electrified ResStock counties show the steepest arms for this reason. The SF sensitivities $m_h$ and $m_c$ remove this size dependence.

The SF sensitivities $m_h$ and $m_c$ group partly by K{\"o}ppen class \cite{beck2018koppen}. The two temperate oceanic (Cfb) aggregates, Hamelin and Kloten, sit at $m_h=0.016$ and $0.018\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$, despite running on water-source and mixed ground-and-air-source heat pumps respectively. The two humid continental (Dfa and Dfb) sites, Oslo and Hennepin, converge on $m_h\approx0.022\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$, even though Oslo's heating load is mostly ground-source and direct resistance, while Hennepin's is air-source. The temperate dry and warm summer (Csb) class, however, splits. Its two measured aggregates, the Washington and Oregon households with ductless HPs, sit at $m_h=0.023$--$0.024\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$, whereas the simulated King County aggregate of ducted HPs with resistance backup reaches $0.036\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$. The cooling sensitivity $m_c$ spans $0.024$--$0.038\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$ without a clear grouping. The hot desert site, Maricopa, is the outlier, with the steepest SF sensitivity of all, $m_h=0.063\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$. This is possibly because a single aggregate and few genuinely cold days leave little data to anchor the coldest end of its heating arm. Overall, the K{\"o}ppen class narrows $m_h$ for aggregates of similar TCL technology. The technology matters as well, and possibly so does the difference between measured and simulated peaks. Across classes, $m_h$ varies by up to a factor of four, so the SF sensitivity is expected to transfer only within a climate class and TCL technology (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). \chg{Section~\ref{sec:transfer-pop} tests this expectation on further populations with complete heating submetering.}

\subsection{Transferability of SF}
\label{sec:transfer}

The SF fit needs submetered TCL data, which a DSO rarely holds for the aggregate under study. The method therefore calibrates the SF sensitivity once on a pilot and reuses it on targets. The pilot is a small set of households whose TCL load is submetered, so that its installed capacity is known. A target is an aggregate whose installed capacity is to be estimated and whose households are not submetered. Pilot and targets belong to the same population, i.e.\ the households of one dataset or study. In the experiments, a split is one random partition of a population into a pilot and targets. Table~\ref{tab:cross} involves no such transfer, since there both fits are made on each dataset's own data.

Only the SF sensitivities $m_h$ and $m_c$ transfer. The pilot's thresholds follow its own building stock and climate, so the target's thresholds $T_h$ and $T_c$ are read from the target's net-load fit instead. The pilot's intercepts $b_h$ and $b_c$ describe weather-independent TCL use, which the target's net-load fit places in $P_{\mathrm{base}}$ and never attributes to its TCL arms.

The transfer rests on a hypothesis, which this paper tests rather than assumes: within a population, the SF sensitivity does not depend on the installed capacity or the TCL penetration of an aggregate. The hypothesis matters because the capacity estimate divides the target's net-load sensitivity by the pilot's SF sensitivity (Section~\ref{sec:uc-capacity}). Any mismatch between the pilot's and the target's SF sensitivity therefore passes directly into the estimated capacity. Three tests follow, each wider than the last. Section~\ref{sec:res-sf} tests the hypothesis within one population, Section~\ref{sec:real-agg} on real aggregates outside the pilot, and Section~\ref{sec:transfer-pop} across populations from five countries.

\section{Aggregated TCL Quantification}
\label{sec:estimation}

The two fits, \eqref{eq:tclfit} and \eqref{eq:sffit}, are complementary. The net-load fit \eqref{eq:tclfit} needs no submetering, but its sensitivities also absorb any temperature response of the non-TCL load. The SF fit \eqref{eq:sffit} needs submetering but isolates the TCL simultaneity. Together they estimate three quantities a DSO needs about the TCLs of an aggregate: their installed capacity, temperature-driven energy and flexibility potential. Installed capacity needs the transferred SF sensitivity, since the net load exposes the aggregated TCL demand, not the non-coincident sum of the individual peaks. The temperature-driven energy follows from \eqref{eq:tclfit} alone. Flexibility needs both. Section~\ref{sec:results} evaluates each quantity.

\subsection{Installed Capacity Estimation}
\label{sec:uc-capacity}

The installed capacity is the sum of the individual peak demands \eqref{eq:pk}, defined per regime. The aggregated TCLs never draw it at once, so the net load never exposes it directly. The net-load fit does expose the temperature-driven TCL load $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\,i}(T)$ of aggregate $i$. Rearranging \eqref{eq:sf-piecewise}, the capacity of regime $k$ follows from this load and the SF at the same temperature.

The net-load arm and the SF must describe the same load. The net-load arm is zero at the aggregate's own threshold by construction, and the weather-independent TCL use sits in $P_{\mathrm{base}}$. The matching SF is therefore the temperature-driven part of \eqref{eq:sffit}. It starts from zero at the aggregate's own thresholds and grows with the pilot's SF sensitivity,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \hat{\mathrm{SF}}_h^{\,i}(T) &= m_h\,(T_h^{\,i} - T), \quad \forall T < T_h^{\,i},\\
    \hat{\mathrm{SF}}_c^{\,i}(T) &= m_c\,(T - T_c^{\,i}), \quad \forall T > T_c^{\,i}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sf-target}
\end{equation}
Dividing the arms of \eqref{eq:tclfit} by \eqref{eq:sf-target} gives the installed capacity of each regime,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \hat{P}_h^{\max,i} &= \frac{s_h^{\,i}\,(T_h^{\,i} - T)}{m_h\,(T_h^{\,i} - T)} = \frac{s_h^{\,i}}{m_h}, \quad \forall T < T_h^{\,i},\\
    \hat{P}_c^{\max,i} &= \frac{s_c^{\,i}\,(T - T_c^{\,i})}{m_c\,(T - T_c^{\,i})} = \frac{s_c^{\,i}}{m_c}, \quad \forall T > T_c^{\,i}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:capacity}
\end{equation}
Note that the temperature cancels, so the estimate needs no choice of evaluation day. The net-load fit supplies the numerator, which the DSO observes. The pilot supplies only the denominator $m_k$. The estimate is therefore as good as these two sensitivities, which Section~\ref{sec:results} tests.

The two regimes are estimated separately. A reversible HP counts in both $\hat{P}_h^{\max}$ and $\hat{P}_c^{\max}$, so the total installed capacity of a population of reversible HPs lies between the larger of the two and their sum.

\subsection{Temperature-Driven Energy Estimation}
\label{sec:uc-energy}

The energy the TCL population draws follows from integrating its temperature response over a certain period.
%In the heating regime, $T < T_h$, the fitted TCL load of Equation~\eqref{eq:tclfit} reduces to its heating arm $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T) = s_h (T_h - T)$.
Over a day of mean temperature $T$, the temperature-driven TCL energy is given by
\begin{equation}
\hat{E}_{\mathrm{TCL}} = \left ( s_h \max(0, T_h - T) + s_c \max(0, T-T_c) \right ) \,\Delta t,
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta t$ being the length of the period, here one day. The annual energy is the sum of \eqref{eq:energy} over the days of the year. No pilot is needed. With the measured daily mean temperature, the fitted TCL load $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)$ separates the daily TCL load from the daily net load. With a forecast temperature, it forecasts that load.

The dead band bounds the estimate. Between $T_h$ and $T_c$ both arms are zero. The estimate therefore misses any TCL energy that persists into mild weather, such as domestic hot water. The estimate captures the temperature-driven share, not the annual total.

\subsection{Flexibility Potential Estimation}
\label{sec:uc-flex}
\label{sec:flex}

A DSO or an aggregator needs to know how much heating energy the TCLs behind a feeder could shift, for congestion management or to offer a flexibility product. This flexibility is the energy the TCL population can shift from its baseline consumption without breaching indoor thermal comfort \cite{Zir19}. How long a shift can last is set by the building stock, through a time window $\Delta t_{Th}$ within which the population must deliver a fixed thermal energy to stay comfortable. A larger $\Delta t_{Th}$ means a better-insulated building, or wider comfort bands \cite{Cha16}. Load-shifting studies of HPs treat this window the same way, as a property of the building \cite{Hon13}. In simulations of UK residential buildings, HP operation could be shifted by 1--2~h in the baseline case and by up to 6~h with much improved insulation and thermal buffering. Building-stock insulation and indoor-comfort data are lacking, so $\Delta t_{Th}$ enters as an exogenous parameter of the building stock, not as an output of the fits.
Over this window, the TCLs must still deliver the temperature-driven energy of \eqref{eq:energy}, drawn at the baseline power $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T) = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}$,
\begin{equation}
\hat{E}_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\Delta t_{Th}} = \hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)\,\Delta t_{Th} = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th},
\label{eq:flex-baseline}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}$ and $\hat{P}^{\max}$ are those of the active regime, from \eqref{eq:sf-target} and \eqref{eq:capacity}. Their product equals the fitted TCL load of \eqref{eq:tclfit}, so the baseline is the temperature-driven load of the net-load fit. The schedule that delivers this same energy while deviating most from the baseline is the two-level square wave of Fig.~\ref{fig:flex-schedule}: full installed power $\hat{P}^{\max}$ for a fraction $\alpha_F$ of the window and zero for the rest. Its energy $\alpha_F\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th}$ must equal \eqref{eq:flex-baseline}, which fixes the duty cycle at the simultaneity factor,
\begin{equation}
\alpha_F\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th} = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th} \;\Longrightarrow\; \alpha_F = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T).
\label{eq:flex-duty}
\end{equation}
The flexible energy is this schedule's symmetric deviation from the baseline. During the on phase the population draws $\hat{P}^{\max} - \hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}$ above baseline for a time $\alpha_F \Delta t_{Th}$; during the off phase it curtails $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}$ for a time $(1 - \alpha_F)\Delta t_{Th}$. The two areas are equal, giving an upward and downward flexible energy
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{flex}} = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\big(1 - \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\big)\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th}.
\label{eq:flex-energy}
\end{equation}
Since $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}$ equals the fitted TCL load, \eqref{eq:flex-energy} equals $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)\,\big(1-\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\big)\,\Delta t_{Th}$. The shiftable load therefore comes from the net-load fit. The transferred SF only sets the headroom above it, $1-\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)$, so the capacity estimate drops out. Since $E_{\mathrm{flex}}$ is linear in $\Delta t_{Th}$, Section~\ref{sec:res-flex} reports it per hour of window, or as ratios in which the window cancels. The instantaneous unused capacity $(1 - \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T))\,\hat{P}^{\max}$ is the special case of the on phase drawn to full power.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\figw]{figures/fig_flex_schedule.pdf}
\vspace{-10pt}
\caption{The flexibility of \eqref{eq:flex-energy} over one cycle of the square-wave schedule. The aggregate must draw the baseline energy $E^{\mathrm{old}}_{\mathrm{TCL}} = \hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)\,\Delta t_{Th}$ (hatched) over the window. It can instead draw the same energy as the full-power pulse $P^{\mathrm{new}}_{\mathrm{TCL}}$ of duty cycle $\alpha_F = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)$. The deviation from baseline is the flexible energy, equal upward ($E_{\mathrm{flex}}^{\uparrow}$) and downward ($E_{\mathrm{flex}}^{\downarrow}$).}
\label{fig:flex-schedule}
\end{figure}

The bound is an idealisation, not a dispatchable schedule. It takes the square wave as feasible and ignores each building's thermal state and the comfort limits within which its TCLs can shift. It therefore stands as a potential against temperature and window length, not as a firm reserve.

\subsection{Kloten Data and Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:data}

The experiment of this section emulates a DSO that submeters one pilot and estimates the installed capacity of many feeders without submetering them. Each semi-synthetic aggregate plays the role of one such feeder. Its net load is known, and so is its true installed capacity, the sum of the capacities of its own HPs. The aggregates differ in size and HP penetration, so each has its own true capacity. For every aggregate, the estimate is its own net-load sensitivity divided by the pilot's SF sensitivity \eqref{eq:capacity}, scored against its true capacity. Fig.~\ref{fig:split} sketches one split.

This section uses a single Swiss population, the households metered at the Kloten weather station in calendar 2023. The data combine two sources, the HEAPO dataset \cite{Bru25} and an additional smart-meter dataset \cite{Kai26b}. The load profiles have 15-minute resolution, while the outdoor temperature is hourly. The only TCLs are HPs, and only heating is observed. The station holds 50 households with a submetered HP and 1307 households with neither an HP nor any other electric heating.

The 50 HPs are split at random into two halves of 25, twenty times over. In each split, the first half is the submetered pilot: its aggregated SF is fitted with \eqref{eq:sffit} and supplies the sensitivity $m_h$. Semi-synthetic aggregates, i.e. sums of measured households, are then drawn from the target half only, so no HP informs both the pilot and a target. Each aggregate combines $N_{hp}$ distinct HPs of that half, with the same households' other load, and $N - N_{hp}$ distinct households without an HP. The household count $N$ takes the values 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50, and $N_{hp}$ the values 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25, with $N_{hp} \le N$. Every feasible cell holds three aggregates per split, giving 1260 aggregates. All draws are without replacement, so no household recurs within an aggregate.

Two error metrics recur below. The weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE) scores an estimate $\hat{y}_i$ against its ground truth $y_i$ across the aggregates $i$,
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{WAPE} = \frac{\sum_i |\hat{y}_i - y_i|}{\sum_i y_i} \times 100\%,
	\label{eq:wape}
\end{equation}
so WAPE weights each aggregate by its true magnitude, not equally. A small aggregate, with a large percentage error but a small absolute error, therefore cannot dominate. The metric reports the total error across a set of aggregates, as a DSO would read it. The median ratio, the median of $\hat{y}_i / y_i$, gives the direction of the error, $< 1$ for an underestimate and $> 1$ for an overestimate.

\subsection{Installed Capacity}
\label{sec:res-capacity}

Equation~\eqref{eq:capacity} estimates the installed HP capacity of each aggregate as the ratio of two sensitivities. The numerator $s_h$ is read from the aggregate's own net-load fit. The denominator $m_h$ is the sensitivity of the pilot of the same split, whose stability Section~\ref{sec:res-sf} examines. The aggregate itself is never submetered.

With the pilot's SF sensitivity, \eqref{eq:capacity} recovers the installed capacity of the 1260 aggregates at a WAPE of 19\%, with a median overestimate of 16\%. In absolute terms, the mean error is 17~kW, the capacity of about three HPs of the mean 6.3~kW. The overestimate is consistent with the non-TCL temperature response that the net-load sensitivity absorbs, the neglected term of \eqref{eq:decomp} discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. The error shrinks as HPs are added: the WAPE falls from 41\% at five HPs to 15\% at twenty-five, and the median overestimate from 35\% to 12\%. This is possibly because the households without an HP, whose temperature response inflates the heating arm, carry less weight as HPs are added.

\subsection{Fit Quality across Spatial and Temporal Aggregation}
\label{sec:res-ff}

Both fits, \eqref{eq:bathtub} and \eqref{eq:sffit}, are made to each aggregate's measurements, varying the number of households and the averaging window. The HP penetration is kept fixed at 50\%, on a separate set of Kloten aggregates, also drawn without replacement. Summing more households evens out their individual, non-thermal behaviour. Averaging over longer windows does the same over time, smoothing out occupancy swings and building thermal inertia that do not follow the temperature hour by hour. \subsection{Temperature-Driven Energy}
\label{sec:res-energy}

Equation~\eqref{eq:energy} needs only the net-load fit, so the energy is the one estimate that requires no pilot. On the days below the threshold, the estimate is compared against the submetered HP energy of each aggregate. Across the 1260 aggregates, the estimate reaches a WAPE of 9\% at a median ratio of 0.97. About 7\% of the annual HP energy falls in the dead band, most plausibly as domestic hot water \cite{Bru23}. Equation~\eqref{eq:energy} omits this energy by construction, so the annual ratio falls to 0.90. Integrating the fitted arm over the hourly temperature, rather than the daily mean, recovers part of this and lifts the annual ratio to 0.94. A day that crosses the threshold holds more heating-degree-hours than its mean implies. This temperature-driven energy is the baseline of the flexibility in Section~\ref{sec:res-flex}.

\subsection{Flexibility Potential}
\label{sec:res-flex}

Over a year, the estimate counts one window per day of the heating regime, below the aggregate's net-load threshold, since the Kloten aggregates have no cooling regime. Each day is evaluated at its daily mean temperature, and the annual flexible energy is the sum of \eqref{eq:flex-energy} over these days. This annual sum is the volume of heating energy that could be shifted over a year, which a DSO or aggregator needs to value a flexibility product. The days of the dead band are left out of the estimate and of its ground truth alike. The estimate therefore covers only temperature-driven flexibility; weather-independent use that could also be shifted, such as hot-water heating, falls outside it. The actual flexibility is computed at hourly resolution: each hour's submetered SF gives $\mathrm{SF}(1-\mathrm{SF})\,P_h^{\max}$ with the true capacity, averaged over the day. The estimate and its ground truth share no input other than the HP load contained in the net load, so their agreement does not follow by construction.

Fig.~\ref{fig:flex-ratio} gives the distribution of the estimated-to-actual ratio of annual flexible energy across the 1260 aggregates. The ratio has a median of 1.04 and an interquartile range of [0.97, 1.17], at a WAPE of 10\%. It splits into two factors. The temperature-driven energy of Section~\ref{sec:res-energy} contributes a median ratio of 0.97, and the unused capacity a median ratio of 1.07. About three percentage points of this factor come from the pilot's SF running slightly below the measured one. About four more come from the daily resolution of the estimate, since the SF swings within the day. The flexibility and energy ratios correlate at 0.96. The flexibility estimate therefore inherits its accuracy mainly from the net-load fit, while the pilot's SF sensitivity shifts the unused capacity by a few percent. Since $\Delta t_{Th}$ cancels in the ratio, neither factor depends on the window.

Computed from the daily mean SF instead, as the estimate is, the actual flexibility would be 3\% higher and the median ratio 1.01, since $\mathrm{SF}(1-\mathrm{SF})$ is concave. The dead-band days, left out by construction, would add a weather-independent floor, most plausibly from hot-water heating. Over the whole year, this floor is worth a median 9\% of the actual flexible energy. On the coldest day, the estimate recovers the flexible energy at a WAPE of 21\% and an $R^2$ of 0.73 against the actual values across aggregates.

\section{Real Aggregates}
\label{sec:validation}

\subsection{\chg{Real Aggregates outside the Pilot}}
\label{sec:real-agg}

Section~\ref{sec:results} tested the method within one population, on aggregates built from its households. This section applies it to real aggregates that no pilot has seen, each summed from its own field meters. Swiss and German aggregates test the heating regime with the Kloten pilot. Submetered data serve only to validate the results, never as input.

Three real aggregates lie outside the Kloten pilot. Two are the HEAPO households of two further Swiss weather stations, Z{\"u}rich-Affoltern (Affoltern below) and Einsiedeln, with 21 and 6 submetered HPs. The third comes from the WPuQ measurement campaign in Hamelin \cite{Sch22}: its net load is the sum of 37 individually metered German single-family houses, each with a submetered HP. All three are summed rather than metered at a transformer, so they carry no network losses. They also contain no households without an HP, which makes their HP penetration 100\%. The pilot now comprises all 50 Kloten HPs. Its SF fit, at the pilot's own net-load threshold, gives $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}_h(T) = 0.029 + 0.018\,(16.6 - T)$, and only its sensitivity $m_h = 0.018\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$ transfers.

Einsiedeln holds only six HPs, below the ten from which Section~\ref{sec:res-capacity} finds the estimates reliable, so its result is indicative. \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Accuracy}
\label{sec:accuracy}

The capacity estimate carries two error sources. The first is the non-TCL temperature response that \eqref{eq:decomp} neglects, which biases the net-load sensitivity upwards. A zero-HP control makes this concrete. Aggregates of 10 to 50 Kloten households without an HP still show a small heating arm, from which \eqref{eq:capacity} infers a median 0.28~kW of spurious capacity per household. This floor is about 4\% of the mean installed capacity of one HP, 6.3~kW. It therefore adds about 45\% to the estimate at 10\% HP penetration, but only about 4\% once every household has an HP.

The second source is the pilot's SF sensitivity. Within the Kloten population, the pilot's SF sensitivity matches the aggregates' own at a median ratio of 1.00, with an interquartile range of $\pm10\%$, so the transfer adds spread rather than bias. \chg{Table~\ref{tab:baselines} sizes the two sources on the Kloten aggregates: with each aggregate's own SF sensitivity, the estimate errs at a WAPE of 14.5\%, and with the pilot's at 19.3\%. On the real aggregates outside the pilot, where every household has an HP,} the estimates lie within 10\% of the true capacity.

\subsection{Generalisation}

The SF sensitivity generalises in a specific and testable way. Within a population, it is stable against the number of HPs and their installed capacity over the tested range, so the SF sensitivity of a submetered pilot serves that population's aggregates. Only this sensitivity transfers. The threshold temperature follows the local building stock and climate, and the net-load fit supplies it from data the DSO already meters. The intercept describes weather-independent TCL use, which lies outside the temperature-driven quantities estimated here. Transferring the full pilot curve instead raises the capacity error to 20\% at Einsiedeln and 26\% at Hamelin.

\chg{The sensitivity itself transfers between populations that share a climate zone and a housing stock. Between random splits of one population, between studies of one climate zone and between countries of one climate class, it stays within 12\% of the target's own (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}). Between heating zones and between social and private housing, however, it differs by factors of 1.7 and 1.65, more than between HP technologies. Across the climate classes of Table~\ref{tab:cross}, it varies by up to a factor of four. Generalisation therefore reduces to transferring a single parameter from a pilot of the same climate zone, TCL technology and housing stock. This mirrors the inverse problem solved for PV installed capacity against irradiance \cite{gouveia2026pvcapacity}, here posed against outdoor temperature.}

\subsection{Data Requirements}

The two fits ask for different data. The net-load fit needs only the aggregated net load and outdoor temperature, both already held by the DSO, and runs on any aggregate. The SF fit needs submetered TCL load and installed capacity, which the DSO rarely holds. A single submetered pilot of the same population calibrates it, and its sensitivity then transfers, so no aggregate under study is submetered. A pilot of a few tens of HPs appears to suffice. A pilot of 25 Kloten HPs reproduces the SF shape of the other 25 at a median $R^2_h$ of 0.91, and recovers their capacity at a median error of $+8\%$ (Section~\ref{sec:res-sf}). The pilot should, however, submeter every electric heating source of its households, including backup resistance heat.

\subsection{Limitations}

The within-population tests keep each pilot apart from its targets. Over twenty random splits of the 50 Kloten HPs, no HP informs both the pilot and a target, and no household recurs within an aggregate. Aggregates of the same split do share HPs, so their errors are not independent, but the twenty splits keep the results from resting on any single partition.

The full estimator is also tested fully out of sample, on three real aggregates whose HPs never enter the pilot, and its estimates lie within 10\% of their true capacity. Table~\ref{tab:transfer} tests the SF transfer further, on populations from five countries. The real aggregates are few and all have an HP in every household, so lower HP penetrations are tested only on the semi-synthetic Kloten aggregates, which span 10\% to 100\%.

The non-TCL temperature response, which the model neglects, is measured rather than assumed away. The zero-HP control of Section~\ref{sec:accuracy} sizes it at a median 0.28~kW of spurious capacity per household. The resulting bias is therefore predictable from the household count the DSO already holds, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} outlines a correction.

The real aggregates also bound the effect of deliberate HP control. Swiss and German domestic HPs commonly run under interruptible tariffs, under which the utility may cut the HP supply during defined blocking periods. Since January 2024, {\S}14a EnWG also lets German DSOs throttle HPs above 4.2~kW \cite{Bna23}. Such control truncates the aggregated TCL peak and may bias the net-load sensitivity and the capacity estimate downwards. On the real aggregates, however, the estimates lie within 10\% of the true capacity, so such control is not a visible source of error there. The WPuQ campaign predates {\S}14a, but blocking-period tariffs do not.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presents a method to estimate the installed capacity of the TCLs behind a feeder from its net load and outdoor temperature. Below a threshold temperature, the daily net load and the SF both rise linearly as the temperature falls. The ratio of the two sensitivities gives the installed capacity, and only the SF sensitivity is calibrated, once, on a small submetered pilot. On 1260 aggregates built from measured Swiss households, the capacity was recovered at a WAPE of 19\%, falling to 12--14\% for aggregates of fifty households at 50\% HP penetration. On three real Swiss and German aggregates where every household has an HP, the estimates came within 10\% of the true capacity. Transferring the pilot's full SF curve, intercept and threshold included, instead erred by up to 26\%. Across submetered households in five countries, the SF sensitivity transferred within one climate zone but not between climate zones or between social and private housing. The pilot should therefore match the target in climate, TCL technology and housing stock. The same fits give the temperature-driven energy without any submetered households, at a WAPE of 9\%, and a flexibility potential at a WAPE of 10\%.

The method thus trades a modest, characterised error for a large gain in deployability. Its estimates are least reliable on aggregates with a handful of HPs and most reliable once tens of HPs are aggregated, which is the regime a substation presents. Where no submetered households are available, the net-load fit alone still gives the temperature-driven energy.

Regarding future work, four directions follow. The submetered populations were small, so a wider and more diverse set of metered HPs, with every electric heating source submetered, would test the transfer on more populations. Such a set would also test the cooling regime, which the method covers but this paper could not evaluate. The only aggregates with complete cooling ground truth, the NEEA households of Washington and Oregon \cite{neea_eulr}, allow no cooling transfer, since the Washington pilot has no day above its cooling threshold in 2023. The net-load sensitivity also absorbs the temperature response of the non-TCL load, which inflates the capacity estimate most where households without TCLs dominate. Future work will compensate the estimates for this response, for instance by subtracting a per-household non-TCL sensitivity scaled by the household count the DSO already holds. It will also evaluate data-driven methods for HP and TCL quantification against the proposed estimator, to see where a learned model earns its data cost and where the proposed estimator suffices. Finally, it will model the TCL consumption that does not depend on temperature, chiefly domestic hot water and baseline operation. The temperature-driven estimate omits this consumption, which sets the gap between the temperature-driven and the annual energy.
