\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The electrification of residential heating and cooling is a central lever of decarbonisation policy. Its devices are thermostatically controlled loads (TCLs), such as heat pumps (HPs), electric resistance heaters and air conditioners, and the HP leads their uptake \cite{Iea22}. In 2025, about 2.9 million HPs were sold in 21 European countries, which brought the installed stock to 29.3 million \cite{Ehp26}. On 200 German low-voltage (LV) feeders, installed HP power rose by over twenty percent from 2023 to 2025 \cite{Tre26}.

The demand of these devices follows the outdoor temperature, and their uptake loads LV networks that may not have been sized for this load. Field and simulation studies show that TCLs raise the coincident peak of feeders and transformers, i.e.\ the highest load their devices draw together, in extreme weather. HPs are the most studied case, with the sharpest peak in cold spells, when many devices run at once \cite{Vel11, Akm14, Som21, Bec24, Lia26}. Electric vehicles (EVs) add further load to the same feeders \cite{Bec24}.

A DSO needs the installed capacity of the TCLs behind each feeder, rather than only their coincident peak, for three reasons. First, the installed capacity scales with adoption, so it supports planning scenarios as the number of TCLs grows. Second, the installed capacity bounds the peak the TCLs can draw when they are synchronised. Third, network planning sizes transformers and cables for a design peak. This design peak is the installed capacity multiplied by the simultaneity factor (SF), the share of the installed capacity drawn at once \cite{Few24}. For HPs, it is set by cold weather, when their coincident demand peaks \cite{Lov17, Che21b, Rou23}. The SF also bounds the flexibility the TCLs can offer \cite{Mul19, Zir19, Dhu15}, since only the capacity not in use can be switched on.

A DSO rarely knows this capacity. Privacy concerns and limits on metering and data exchange restrict the DSO's access to household data, and its records of distributed energy resources (DERs) are often incomplete or out of date \cite{Gou26}. What the DSO does hold is the net load metered for a collection of households, such as those supplied by a substation. This paper calls such a metered point, together with the households behind it, an aggregate. Where smart meters are rolled out, the DSO also meters each household, but its access to these data is often restricted. The DSO usually knows how many households each feeder serves \cite{Tre26}. The installed capacity of the TCLs behind a feeder therefore stays largely unobserved.

\input{tables/tab_litreview}

Five lines of research address this unobserved capacity, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:litreview}. Non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM) and HP detection recover thermostatic load from customer meters, either as a present-absent label \cite{Fei13, Wei20b, Ray19} or as a disaggregated energy time series \cite{Bru23, Gis26}. Some of these methods extend to LV substations \cite{Wan20, Oma24}, validated mostly on synthetic rather than measured substation aggregates. They need labelled or submetered training data, however, and stop short of the installed capacity.

Change-point and degree-day models fit load as a piecewise-linear function of temperature, from building-level formulations \cite{Fel08, Ruc92, Kis04}, reviewed in \cite{Faz16}, to aggregated and smart-meter populations \cite{Ali11, Per17b, Mac19}. They recover heating and cooling sensitivities and a threshold temperature without any labels. They stop, however, at the coincident response and never reach the installed capacity, the sum of the individual peak demands.

A separate body of work models aggregated TCL populations for control. These models describe a population through the thermal parameters of its devices. Callaway \cite{Cal09} uses such a model to make a population follow a load signal, and Hao et al.\ \cite{Hao15} represent a population as a stochastic battery whose power and energy limits bound its flexibility. The state of such a population can be estimated from its aggregate power \cite{Mat13}, and the air-conditioning demand of a feeder can be disaggregated in real time from feeder measurements \cite{Led18}. These methods assume known device models, or learn them from building- and device-level data, and none estimates how much TCL capacity is installed behind the meter.

For photovoltaics (PV) and other DERs, by contrast, installed-capacity estimation is well developed. Methods infer the capacity from aggregated net load or smart-meter data \cite{Was21, Liu22, Gou25, gouveia2026pvcapacity}. Irradiance plays the role there that temperature could play for TCLs, yet the TCL counterpart is largely missing \cite{Gou26}.

Studies of HP demand measure the coincident peak, as an after-diversity maximum demand, or model the coincidence factor, on submetered populations of known size \cite{Lov17, Che21b, Rou23}. Closest to this paper, Anderson et al.\ \cite{And20} fit a probabilistic model of HP demand against temperature to submetered customer data, to assess LV networks under a given HP penetration. Such models run forward, from a known number of HPs to the demand they cause, and none recovers the installed capacity behind a feeder whose households are not submetered.

To the best of the authors' knowledge, no prior method estimates the installed capacity of an aggregated TCL population from feeder-level net load without submetering the households of the target feeder. Change-point models observe the right signal but stop at the coincident response, and simultaneity studies reach the installed capacity only on submetered populations.

This paper joins the change-point and simultaneity approaches. It estimates the installed capacity from the net load and temperature of a feeder, together with the SF sensitivity, i.e.\ how fast the SF grows as the temperature falls. The SF sensitivity is calibrated once on a small pilot. The pilot is a set of households whose heating is submetered, i.e.\ metered separately from the rest of their load, so that their installed heating capacity can be measured. On the feeder under study, called the target, the net load also grows as the temperature falls, at a rate the DSO reads from its own data. This rate, the net-load sensitivity, is larger the more TCL capacity is installed behind the feeder. Dividing the net-load sensitivity of the target by the SF sensitivity of the pilot therefore gives the installed capacity of the target. The households of the target are never submetered, so one pilot serves many feeders.

The contributions of this paper are threefold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Installed Capacity Estimation:} A method to estimate the installed TCL capacity behind a feeder from its net load and temperature, calibrated once on a small submetered pilot. The households of the target feeder are never submetered. On 1260 semi-synthetic Swiss aggregates, the capacity is recovered at an nMAE of 9.5\%, or 7.5\% when the total number of households is known.
\item \emph{SF Sensitivity Transfer:} An evaluation of how far the SF sensitivity of a pilot transfers across populations. It holds at a median ratio of 1.00 between aggregates of one population, and within 10\% between cities, 12\% between countries and 14\% between HP technologies. It differs by 1.65-fold between housing tenures and 1.7-fold between climate zones.
\item \emph{Flexibility Potential Estimation:} A method to quantify the flexibility potential of an aggregated TCL population against temperature, from the net-load sensitivity and the SF sensitivity. On the semi-synthetic Swiss aggregates, it recovers the annual flexible energy at an nMAE of 4.8\%.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem and Approach}
\label{sec:problem}
\label{sec:fits}

For the TCLs within an aggregate, this paper estimates two quantities: their installed capacity and their flexibility potential. Both need the net load of the aggregate and the outdoor temperature, held by the DSO, and the SF sensitivity, calibrated once on a submetered pilot (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). The flexibility builds on the temperature-driven TCL energy, which the net-load fit gives without a pilot.

The net load is the sum of a TCL and a non-TCL component,
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{net}}(t) = P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(t) + P_{\text{non-TCL}}(t),
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
where $t$ denotes time and only the TCL component responds directly to temperature. The non-TCL component also carries a weaker, indirect temperature dependence, visible in the whole-home energy signature \cite{Wes20}. Still, TCLs dominate the total temperature response, so the model neglects the non-TCL contribution. On average, each household adds small non-TCL sensitivities $s_{n,h}$ and $s_{n,c}$ [kW/$^\circ$C] to the heating and cooling arms, so the neglected term grows with the household count $N$ and weighs most where few households hold a TCL. Section~\ref{sec:uc-capacity} gives an optional correction, and Section~\ref{sec:res-capacity} sizes the effect.

Two fits describe how the TCLs of an aggregate respond to the outdoor temperature. The first, the net-load fit, uses only the net load and temperature the DSO holds (Section~\ref{sec:fits-net}). The second, the SF fit, needs the submetered TCL load and installed capacity, which in practice only a pilot provides (Section~\ref{sec:fits-sf}). Both fits share the same piecewise-linear form and use daily means, which reduce the effect of building thermal inertia \cite{Per17b}.

\subsection{Net-Load Fit}
\label{sec:fits-net}


When an aggregate's net load is averaged over each day and plotted against the daily mean temperature, it follows a piecewise-linear pattern. The load is roughly flat on mild days, and rises close to linearly as the temperature falls below, or rises above, a threshold.
%(ADD the table here we discussed)
 The net-load fit expresses the aggregated net load of \eqref{eq:decomp} as
\begin{equation}
\hat{P}_{\mathrm{net}}(T) = P_{\mathrm{base}} + s_h \max(0, T_h - T) + s_c \max(0, T - T_c),
\label{eq:bathtub}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the daily mean temperature [$^\circ$C], $P_{\mathrm{base}}$ the temperature-independent load [kW] and $s_h$ and $s_c$ the heating and cooling sensitivities [kW/$^\circ$C]. The heating and cooling threshold temperatures $T_h \le T_c$ [$^\circ$C], also known as balance-point temperatures, bound a dead band over which no thermal response is present. The parameters of the fitted curve are computed through Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), with $T_h$ and $T_c$ chosen as the values that minimise the squared residuals of the entire fit. The two temperature-dependent segments, called the heating and cooling arms of the fit, form the fitted aggregated TCL load.

Fig.~\ref{fig:austin}(a) shows the net-load fit to aggregated Austin smart-meter data \cite{pecanstreet}, with the parameters of \eqref{eq:bathtub} marked. The thresholds divide the temperature axis into three regimes: the heating regime below $T_h$, the cooling regime above $T_c$ and the dead band between them, where the population neither heats nor cools. This work characterises the heating and cooling regimes alike and leaves the dead band out of scope. The evaluation of Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:validation} covers the heating regime only, since the available data allow no test of the transfer in the cooling regime (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\end{equation}

The fitted parameters carry a physical interpretation. The threshold temperatures $T_h$ and $T_c$ are behavioural. They mark where the population, on average, begins to heat or cool, rather than being a property of any building or device. Individual households start heating or cooling at different temperatures. Once many are aggregated, these differences average out, and $T_h$ and $T_c$ settle at values that reflect regional preferences for starting space heating or cooling (Section~\ref{sec:cross}).

The sensitivities $s_h$ and $s_c$ of the net-load fit measure how much heating or cooling load the population adds per degree.
% A steeper arm signals more installed TCLs.
They scale with the TCL capacity installed behind the meter, so an aggregate with more TCLs shows a steeper arm, all else being equal. Between aggregates of the same size, a steeper arm therefore also indicates a higher TCL penetration. Section~\ref{sec:res-sf} confirms this, with $s_h$ tracking installed capacity across the aggregates.

The balance between $s_h$ and $s_c$ reflects the local climate. Hot regions are expected to be cooling-dominated, with $s_h \approx 0$, whereas regions with long winters, such as the Nordic countries, are heating-dominated, with $s_c \approx 0$.



\subsection{Simultaneity-Factor Fit}
\label{sec:fits-sf}

% \textcolor{red}{
% What is simultaneity factor?
% why is it important?
% }


The SF is the ratio of the demand of a group of loads, here TCLs, at a given instant to the sum of their individual maximum demands. It reflects that connected devices do not all draw their full power at the same instant \cite{fani2024impact}. In distribution networks, the SF is central to planning. It lets utilities size transformers, cables and protection for the peak the group actually draws, rather than for the sum of individual peaks.


% The second function characterises the synchronous behaviour, or simultaneity, of the TCL population.
Mathematically, the SF is the share of the aggregate's installed capacity drawn at a given temperature $T$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SF}(T) = \frac{P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)}{P_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\max}},
\label{eq:sf}
\end{equation}
where $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)$ is the daily mean TCL load on a day of mean temperature $T$, and $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\max}$ is the total installed capacity of the TCLs in the aggregate. Nameplate ratings are not available for most households, so the installed capacity of each TCL is taken as the peak of its submetered 15-min draw. The installed capacity of an aggregate is thus the summed peak draw of its TCLs, which tracks their rated power.

\chg{Since $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)$ is a daily mean, the SF of this paper is a daily-mean SF. It is lower than the SF used in network planning, which takes the 15-min interval of highest aggregate demand; Section~\ref{sec:res-sf} gives the ratio between the two. The daily mean suits the aim of this paper, since the installed capacity follows from the SF sensitivity, and daily means reduce the effect of building thermal inertia \cite{Per17b}.}
% \textcolor{blue}{Since $P_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\max}$ is assumed to be known for  }
% , or a proxy such as the summed peak of the individual TCLs.

An aggregate may have a mix of TCLs with different behaviours with respect to temperature. Some TCLs, such as reversible HPs, can both heat and cool. Others only heat, such as resistive heaters, or only cool, such as air conditioners. Using the total TCL installed capacity in \eqref{eq:sf} would lead to an underestimation of the SF, since the installed capacity of cooling-exclusive devices would still count towards the SF in the heating regime, and \emph{vice-versa}. Therefore, each regime will have its own capacity, which is defined as
\begin{equation}
    P_k^{\max} = \sum_{n\in \mathcal{N}_k}P_n^{\max},
\label{eq:pk}
\end{equation}
where $k$ denotes the regime (heating or cooling), $\mathcal{N}_k$ is the set of TCLs active in regime $k$, and $P_n^{\max}$ is the installed capacity of TCL $n$. The measured SF is then defined per regime as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{SF}_h(T) &= {P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)}/{P_h^{\max}}, \quad \forall  T < T_h,\\
    \mathrm{SF}_c(T) &= {P_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)}/{P_c^{\max}}, \quad \forall T > T_c,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sf-piecewise}
\end{equation}

with $h$ denoting heating and $c$ cooling. The SF is by definition bounded in $[0,1]$. The SF fit takes the same form as \eqref{eq:bathtub},
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \hat{\mathrm{SF}}_h(T) &= b_h + m_h (T_h - T), \quad \forall T < T_h,\\
    \hat{\mathrm{SF}}_c(T) &= b_c + m_c ( T - T_c), \quad \forall T > T_c,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sffit}
\end{equation}
where $T_h$ and $T_c$ are the thresholds of the net-load fit \eqref{eq:bathtub} of the same aggregate. The SF fit does not fit thresholds of its own. It borrows them from the net-load fit, so both fits describe the same heating and cooling regimes. The intercepts $b_h$ and $b_c$ and the heating and cooling SF sensitivities $m_h$ and $m_c$ [1/$^\circ$C] are determined by OLS on the days of each regime. The intercepts capture the weather-independent share of TCL use, such as domestic hot water, which the net-load fit instead places in $P_{\mathrm{base}}$. The SF fit therefore needs three inputs for the same aggregate: its daily submetered TCL load, its installed TCL capacity per regime and the daily mean temperature. Its thresholds come from the aggregate's own net-load fit. In practice, only a submetered pilot provides the first two (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). Fig.~\ref{fig:austin}(b) shows the SF data points and the SF fit for the same Austin population.

The SF fit gives the SF at any temperature of its regime, which links it to the design peak of Section~\ref{sec:intro}. At a design temperature $T_{\mathrm{d}} < T_h$, the design SF is $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}_h(T_{\mathrm{d}}) = b_h + m_h (T_h - T_{\mathrm{d}})$, and the design peak is the installed capacity multiplied by this SF. Since the SF of this paper is a daily mean, this product is a daily-mean design load. The 15-min design peak lies above it by the peak-to-mean ratio of Section~\ref{sec:res-sf}.


%\textcolor{red}{where do you define how to calculate $m_h, m_c$}

The observed SF stays well below 1. Each arm would reach the installed capacity of its regime, with every TCL drawing at once, only at temperatures well outside any observed weather. The gap between the observed TCL load and the installed capacity, the unused share $1-\mathrm{SF}$ of that capacity, is what the flexibility of Section~\ref{sec:flex} draws on.

\subsection{Cross-dataset characterisation}
\label{sec:cross}

\input{tables/tab_cross}

Both fits hold across climates. Table~\ref{tab:cross} reports them on aggregates from four countries, with each climate given as its K{\"o}ppen--Geiger class \cite{beck2018koppen}. The heating regime, the cooling regime or both are resolved as the local climate dictates (Fig.~\ref{fig:austin}(a)). Each fit is scored by its coefficient of determination on the days of its own regime, $R^2_h$ for heating and $R^2_c$ for cooling. In the heating regime, the net-load fit reaches an $R^2_h$ between 0.68 and 0.92 across the year-round aggregates. $R^2_h$ is lowest in Austin, where electric heating is marginal. In the cooling regime, $R^2_c$ ranges from 0.95 in hot Maricopa to 0.75 in mild Oregon, where cooling is rare.

The threshold temperatures follow the local climate. $T_h$ falls between 8 and 17\,$^\circ$C, and $T_c$ between 18 and 26\,$^\circ$C. A hot county such as Maricopa has a steep cooling arm, whereas a cold one such as Hamelin has no cooling whatsoever. The sensitivities $s_h$ and $s_c$ of the net-load fit scale with the aggregate's total installed TCL, and therefore with its size. The 849-household Norwegian aggregate and the electrified ResStock counties show the steepest arms for this reason. The SF sensitivities $m_h$ and $m_c$ remove this size dependence.

The SF sensitivities $m_h$ and $m_c$ group partly by K{\"o}ppen class \cite{beck2018koppen}. The two temperate oceanic (Cfb) aggregates, Hamelin and Kloten, sit at $m_h=0.016$ and $0.018\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$, despite running on water-source and mixed ground-and-air-source heat pumps respectively. The two humid continental (Dfa and Dfb) sites, Oslo and Hennepin, converge on $m_h\approx0.022\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$, even though Oslo's heating load is mostly ground-source and direct resistance, while Hennepin's is air-source. The temperate dry and warm summer (Csb) class, however, splits. Its two measured aggregates, the Washington and Oregon households with ductless HPs, sit at $m_h=0.023$--$0.024\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$, whereas the simulated King County aggregate of ducted HPs with resistance backup reaches $0.036\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$. The cooling sensitivity $m_c$ spans $0.024$--$0.038\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$ without a clear grouping. The hot desert site, Maricopa, is the outlier, with the steepest SF sensitivity of all, $m_h=0.063\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$. This is possibly because a single aggregate and few genuinely cold days leave little data to anchor the coldest end of its heating arm. Overall, the K{\"o}ppen class narrows $m_h$ for aggregates of similar TCL technology. The technology matters as well, and possibly so does the difference between measured and simulated peaks. Across classes, $m_h$ varies by up to a factor of four, so the SF sensitivity is expected to transfer only within a climate class and TCL technology (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). \chg{Section~\ref{sec:transfer-pop} tests this expectation on further populations with complete heating submetering.}

\subsection{Transferability of SF}
\label{sec:transfer}

The method calibrates the SF sensitivity once on a submetered pilot and reuses it on every target. The SF fit needs submetered TCL data, which a DSO rarely holds for the aggregate under study. The pilot is a small set of households whose TCL load is submetered, so that its installed capacity is known. A target is an aggregate whose installed capacity is to be estimated and whose households are not submetered. Pilot and targets belong to the same population, i.e.\ the households of one dataset or study. In the experiments, a split is one random partition of a population into a pilot and targets. Table~\ref{tab:cross} involves no such transfer, since there both fits are made on each dataset's own data.

Only the SF sensitivities $m_h$ and $m_c$ transfer. The pilot's thresholds follow its own building stock and climate, so the target's thresholds $T_h$ and $T_c$ are read from the target's net-load fit instead. The pilot's intercepts $b_h$ and $b_c$ describe weather-independent TCL use, which the target's net-load fit places in $P_{\mathrm{base}}$ and never attributes to its TCL arms.

The transfer rests on a hypothesis, which this paper tests rather than assumes: within a population, the SF sensitivity does not depend on the installed capacity or the TCL penetration of an aggregate. The hypothesis matters because the capacity estimate divides the target's net-load sensitivity by the pilot's SF sensitivity (Section~\ref{sec:uc-capacity}). Any mismatch between the pilot's and the target's SF sensitivity therefore passes directly into the estimated capacity. Three tests follow, each wider than the last. Section~\ref{sec:res-sf} tests the hypothesis within one population, Section~\ref{sec:real-agg} on aggregates outside the pilot, and Section~\ref{sec:transfer-pop} across populations from five countries.

\section{Aggregated TCL Quantification}
\label{sec:estimation}

The two fits, \eqref{eq:tclfit} and \eqref{eq:sffit}, are complementary. The net-load fit \eqref{eq:tclfit} needs no submetering, but its sensitivities also absorb any temperature response of the non-TCL load. The SF fit \eqref{eq:sffit} needs submetering but isolates the TCL simultaneity. Together they estimate two quantities a DSO needs about the TCLs of an aggregate: their installed capacity and flexibility potential. Installed capacity needs the transferred SF sensitivity, since the net load exposes the aggregated TCL demand, not the non-coincident sum of the individual peaks. Flexibility needs both fits: the net-load fit gives the temperature-driven energy the TCLs must deliver, and the SF fit the headroom above it. Section~\ref{sec:results} evaluates each quantity.

\subsection{Installed Capacity Estimation}
\label{sec:uc-capacity}

The installed capacity of each regime is the target's net-load sensitivity divided by the pilot's SF sensitivity, $s_k/m_k$. It is the sum of the individual peak demands \eqref{eq:pk}, which the aggregated TCLs never draw at once, so the net load never exposes it directly. The net-load fit does expose the temperature-driven TCL load $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\,i}(T)$ of aggregate $i$. Rearranging \eqref{eq:sf-piecewise}, the capacity of regime $k$ follows from this load and the SF at the same temperature.

The net-load arm and the SF must describe the same load. The net-load arm is zero at the aggregate's own threshold by construction, and the weather-independent TCL use sits in $P_{\mathrm{base}}$. The matching SF is therefore the temperature-driven part of \eqref{eq:sffit}. It starts from zero at the aggregate's own thresholds and grows with the pilot's SF sensitivity,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \hat{\mathrm{SF}}_h^{\,i}(T) &= m_h\,(T_h^{\,i} - T), \quad \forall T < T_h^{\,i},\\
    \hat{\mathrm{SF}}_c^{\,i}(T) &= m_c\,(T - T_c^{\,i}), \quad \forall T > T_c^{\,i}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sf-target}
\end{equation}
Dividing the arms of \eqref{eq:tclfit} by \eqref{eq:sf-target} gives the installed capacity of each regime,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \hat{P}_h^{\max,i} &= \frac{s_h^{\,i}\,(T_h^{\,i} - T)}{m_h\,(T_h^{\,i} - T)} = \frac{s_h^{\,i}}{m_h}, \quad \forall T < T_h^{\,i},\\
    \hat{P}_c^{\max,i} &= \frac{s_c^{\,i}\,(T - T_c^{\,i})}{m_c\,(T - T_c^{\,i})} = \frac{s_c^{\,i}}{m_c}, \quad \forall T > T_c^{\,i}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:capacity}
\end{equation}
Note that the temperature cancels, so the estimate needs no choice of evaluation day. The net-load fit supplies the numerator, which the DSO observes. The pilot supplies only the denominator $m_k$. The estimate is therefore as good as these two sensitivities, which Section~\ref{sec:results} tests.

The net-load sensitivities also hold the non-TCL response of the households, about $N s_{n,k}$ in regime $k$ (Section~\ref{sec:problem}), which biases \eqref{eq:capacity} upwards. A DSO that holds the net load of an aggregate without TCLs, for instance a feeder without HPs or air conditioners, can fit \eqref{eq:bathtub} to it at the target's thresholds and divide its sensitivities by its household count. This gives the non-TCL sensitivities per household, $s_{n,h}$ and $s_{n,c}$, which the corrected capacity of each regime subtracts,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \hat{P}_h^{\max,i} &= \frac{s_h^{\,i} - N^i s_{n,h}}{m_h}, & \hat{P}_c^{\max,i} &= \frac{s_c^{\,i} - N^i s_{n,c}}{m_c},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:capacity-corr}
\end{equation}
where $N^i$ is the household count of aggregate $i$. On a target aggregate, the correction needs only its household count $N^i$, not which of its households hold a TCL.

\subsection{Flexibility Potential Estimation}
\label{sec:uc-flex}
\label{sec:flex}

The flexibility potential is the energy the TCL population can shift from its baseline consumption without breaching indoor thermal comfort \cite{Zir19}. Over a window $\Delta t_{Th}$, it equals $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\big(1-\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\big)\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th}$ \eqref{eq:flex-energy}. A DSO or an aggregator needs this energy for congestion management or to offer a flexibility product.

The baseline is the temperature-driven TCL energy, which follows from the net-load fit alone, with no pilot. Over a day of mean temperature $T$, it is given by
\begin{equation}
\hat{E}_{\mathrm{TCL}} = \left ( s_h \max(0, T_h - T) + s_c \max(0, T-T_c) \right ) \,\Delta t,
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta t$ being the length of the period, here one day. Between $T_h$ and $T_c$ both arms are zero, so the baseline misses any TCL energy that persists into mild weather, such as domestic hot water.

How long a shift can last is set by the building stock, through a time window $\Delta t_{Th}$ within which the population must deliver a fixed thermal energy to stay comfortable. A larger $\Delta t_{Th}$ means a better-insulated building, or wider comfort bands \cite{Cha16}. Load-shifting studies of HPs treat this window the same way, as a property of the building \cite{Hon13}. In simulations of UK residential buildings, HP operation could be shifted by 1--2~h in the baseline case and by up to 6~h with much improved insulation and thermal buffering. Building-stock insulation and indoor-comfort data are lacking, so $\Delta t_{Th}$ enters as an exogenous parameter of the building stock, held constant across temperatures, not as an output of the fits.

Over this window, the TCLs must still deliver the temperature-driven energy of \eqref{eq:energy}, drawn at the baseline power $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T) = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}$,
\begin{equation}
\hat{E}_{\mathrm{TCL}}^{\Delta t_{Th}} = \hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)\,\Delta t_{Th} = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th},
\label{eq:flex-baseline}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}$ and $\hat{P}^{\max}$ are those of the active regime, from \eqref{eq:sf-target} and \eqref{eq:capacity}. Their product equals the fitted TCL load of \eqref{eq:tclfit}, so the baseline is the temperature-driven load of the net-load fit, and weather-independent TCL use, such as hot-water heating, lies outside it. The schedule that delivers this same energy while deviating most from the baseline is the two-level square wave of Fig.~\ref{fig:flex-schedule}: full installed power $\hat{P}^{\max}$ for a fraction $\alpha_F$ of the window and zero for the rest. Its energy $\alpha_F\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th}$ must equal \eqref{eq:flex-baseline}, which fixes the duty cycle at the simultaneity factor,
\begin{equation}
\alpha_F\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th} = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th} \;\Longrightarrow\; \alpha_F = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T).
\label{eq:flex-duty}
\end{equation}
The flexible energy is this schedule's symmetric deviation from the baseline. During the on phase the population draws $\hat{P}^{\max} - \hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}$ above baseline for a time $\alpha_F \Delta t_{Th}$; during the off phase it curtails $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}$ for a time $(1 - \alpha_F)\Delta t_{Th}$. The two areas are equal, giving an upward and downward flexible energy
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{flex}} = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\big(1 - \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\big)\,\hat{P}^{\max}\,\Delta t_{Th}.
\label{eq:flex-energy}
\end{equation}

The shiftable load comes from the net-load fit, and the transferred SF sets only the headroom above it, $1-\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)$. Since $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\,\hat{P}^{\max}$ equals the fitted TCL load, \eqref{eq:flex-energy} equals $\hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)\,\big(1-\hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)\big)\,\Delta t_{Th}$, so the capacity estimate drops out. Since $E_{\mathrm{flex}}$ is linear in $\Delta t_{Th}$, Section~\ref{sec:res-flex} reports it per hour of window, or as ratios in which the window cancels.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\figw]{figures/fig_flex_schedule.pdf}
\vspace{-10pt}
\caption{The flexibility of \eqref{eq:flex-energy} over one cycle of the square-wave schedule. The aggregate must draw the baseline energy $E^{\mathrm{old}}_{\mathrm{TCL}} = \hat{P}_{\mathrm{TCL}}(T)\,\Delta t_{Th}$ (hatched) over the window. It can instead draw the same energy as the full-power pulse $P^{\mathrm{new}}_{\mathrm{TCL}}$ of duty cycle $\alpha_F = \hat{\mathrm{SF}}(T)$. The bound is an idealisation, not a dispatchable schedule. It takes the square wave as feasible and ignores each building's thermal state and the comfort limits within which its TCLs can shift. It therefore stands as a potential against temperature and window length, not as a firm reserve.

\section{Numerical Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Kloten Data and Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:data}

The experiment of this section emulates a DSO that submeters one pilot and estimates the installed capacity of many feeders without submetering them. Each semi-synthetic aggregate plays the role of one such feeder. Its net load is known, and so is its true installed capacity, the sum of the capacities of its own HPs. The aggregates differ in size and HP penetration, so each has its own true capacity. For every aggregate, the estimate is its own net-load sensitivity divided by the pilot's SF sensitivity \eqref{eq:capacity}, scored against its true capacity. Fig.~\ref{fig:split} sketches one split.

This section uses a single Swiss population, the households metered at the Kloten weather station in calendar 2023. The data combine two sources, the HEAPO dataset \cite{Bru25} and an additional smart-meter dataset \cite{Kai26b}. The load profiles have 15-minute resolution, while the outdoor temperature is hourly. The only TCLs are HPs, and only heating is observed. The station holds 50 households with a submetered HP and 1307 households with neither an HP nor any other electric heating.

The 50 HPs are split at random into two halves of 25, twenty times over. In each split, the first half is the submetered pilot: its aggregated SF is fitted with \eqref{eq:sffit} and supplies the sensitivity $m_h$. Semi-synthetic aggregates, i.e. sums of measured households, are then drawn from the target half only, so no HP informs both the pilot and a target. Each aggregate combines $N_{hp}$ distinct HPs of that half, with the same households' other load, and $N - N_{hp}$ distinct households without an HP. The household count $N$ takes the values 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50, and $N_{hp}$ the values 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25, with $N_{hp} \le N$. Every feasible cell holds three aggregates per split, giving 1260 aggregates. All draws are without replacement, so no household recurs within an aggregate.

Two error metrics recur below. The normalised mean absolute error (nMAE) scores an estimate $\hat{y}_i$ against its ground truth $y_i$ across a set of $n$ aggregates,
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{nMAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \frac{|\hat{y}_i - y_i|}{\max_j y_j} \times 100\%,
	\label{eq:nmae}
\end{equation}
where the maximum is taken over the same set. Each error is thus a percentage error scaled by the ratio of its aggregate's true value to the largest in the set. A small aggregate, with a large percentage error but a small absolute error, therefore cannot dominate. The median ratio, the median of $\hat{y}_i / y_i$, gives the direction of the error, $< 1$ for an underestimate and $> 1$ for an overestimate. Ranges are 10--90\% ranges across aggregates, splits or bootstrap samples.

\subsection{Installed Capacity}
\label{sec:res-capacity}

With the pilot's SF sensitivity, \eqref{eq:capacity} recovers the installed capacity of the 1260 aggregates at an nMAE of 9.5\%, with a median overestimate of 16\%. In absolute terms, the mean error is 17~kW, the capacity of about three HPs of the mean 6.3~kW. The overestimate comes from the non-TCL temperature response that the net-load sensitivity absorbs, the neglected term of \eqref{eq:decomp} discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. The error shrinks as HPs are added: the nMAE falls from 22\% at five HPs to 13\% at twenty-five, and the median overestimate from 35\% to 12\%.

Fig.~\ref{fig:capacity-error} shows the ratio of estimated to true capacity against the HP penetration. Its median falls from 1.66 at 10\% penetration to between 1.07 and 1.13 from 50\% on, and its 10--90\% range narrows from 1.30--2.21 to about 0.9--1.3 above 50\%. Below 15\% penetration, even the 10th percentile overestimates the capacity, by 5--30\%.

The overestimate grows as the penetration falls because $s_h$ also holds the non-TCL response of every household, about $N s_{n,h}$, while the TCL response grows only with $N_{hp}$. The pilot is not the cause: with each aggregate's own $m_h$, the median ratio still falls from 1.57 at 10\% penetration to 1.06 at 100\%. Fitted on 50 households without an HP that no aggregate of the split contains, $s_{n,h}$ is a median 0.005~kW/$^\circ$C, against 0.112~kW/$^\circ$C for one HP. The correction \eqref{eq:capacity-corr} then cuts the median ratio at 10\% penetration from 1.66 to 1.19 and holds it within 1.00--1.11 from 15\% on. It lowers the nMAE from 9.5\% to 7.5\%. It removes most of the bias but not the spread, since a tenth of the households without an HP give 81\% of their response. The calibration households must hold no TCL: the steeper sensitivity of the pilot's other load, 0.011~kW/$^\circ$C, leaves the estimate a third too low at low penetration.

\subsection{Why the Estimate Works: Invariance and Transfer}
\label{sec:res-sf}

The capacity estimate divides a sensitivity that grows with the installed capacity by one that does not. This subsection shows both properties on the Kloten aggregates, which is the within-population test of the hypothesis of Section~\ref{sec:transfer}.

The SF fit is stable within the population. Because the SF comes from the submetered HPs alone, the households without an HP do not affect it. Fig.~\ref{fig:slope-capacity}(b) therefore plots the own $m_h$ of every aggregate against its installed capacity. It holds near $0.018\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$ at every capacity, at a Spearman correlation of $-0.06$, and the maximum heating SF holds near 0.47.
%They differ slightly because they are fitted differently: $m$ as the median of the per-substation SF fits, and the slope as a single regression of $s_h$ on installed capacity pooled across the substations.
More HPs behind the meter do not shift these values but tighten them, as the 10--90\% range of $m_h$ narrows from 0.0092 at five HPs to 0.0023 at twenty-five. \chg{This maximum is a daily mean. Taken at the 15-min peak, as network planning takes it, the SF of the 50 Kloten HPs reaches 0.56 on the coldest day and 0.59 over the year. Both lie above the HP SF of 0.425 that a Great Britain-wide LV network study draws from UK field trials \cite{Few24}. On the coldest day of the Kloten data, the 15-min peak SF of the 50 submetered HPs is 1.28 times the daily mean, and on a median heating day 1.75 times.}


Across the twenty splits, the pilot's SF sensitivity $m_h$ has a median of $0.018\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$ and a 10--90\% range of 0.017--0.019. In one split, the pilot's SF fit at its own net-load threshold gives $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}_h(T) = 0.021 + 0.019\,(16.6 - T)$ for $T<16.6\,^\circ$C, at an $R^2_h$ of 0.94.

Placed at the target half's net-load threshold, the pilot's SF curve also reproduces the SF of the whole target half, at a median $R^2_h$ of 0.91 over 120 random splits and a 10--90\% range of 0.77--0.95. The pilot's SF sensitivity also recovers the capacity of the target half through \eqref{eq:capacity}. The median error is $+8\%$, with a 10--90\% range from $-8\%$ to $+27\%$.

The net-load sensitivity, in turn, grows with the installed capacity. Fig.~\ref{fig:slope-capacity}(a) plots the fitted $s_h$ against the installed HP capacity across the aggregates, coloured by the number of HPs. The two track closely, at a Pearson correlation of 0.97, along a line through the origin of slope $0.020\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$. A steeper heating arm therefore signals more installed capacity, as Section~\ref{sec:fits-net} anticipated.

\subsection{Flexibility Potential}
\label{sec:res-flex}

The flexibility estimate recovers the annual flexible energy at an nMAE of 4.8\%, but its error changes sign with temperature. It overestimates the flexibility on the coldest days and underestimates it in mild weather, and both errors come mainly from the net-load fit, not from the transferred SF.

Across the 1260 aggregates, the ratio of estimated to actual annual flexible energy has a median of 1.04 and a 10--90\% range of [0.92, 1.35], at an nMAE of 4.8\%. The estimate sums \eqref{eq:flex-energy} over the heating-regime days, each at its daily mean temperature, since the Kloten aggregates have no cooling regime. The actual covers the same days and applies the same expression to each hour's submetered SF and the true capacity, averaged over the day. The two share no input other than the HP load in the net load, so their agreement does not follow by construction.

The ratio splits into two factors, and the net-load fit sets most of it. The first is the baseline energy \eqref{eq:energy}, which the net-load fit gives with no pilot. Against the submetered HP energy on the days below the threshold, it has a median ratio of 0.97, at an nMAE of 4.1\%. The second, the unused capacity, has a median ratio of 1.07. About three percentage points of this factor come from the pilot's SF running slightly below the measured one. About four more come from the daily resolution of the estimate: computed from the daily mean SF, the actual would be 3\% higher and the median ratio 1.01, since $\mathrm{SF}(1-\mathrm{SF})$ is concave. The flexibility ratio and the energy factor correlate at 0.96. Since $\Delta t_{Th}$ cancels in the ratio, neither factor depends on the window.

The annual ratio of 1.04 averages two opposite errors across temperature (Fig.~\ref{fig:flex-temperature}). The estimate overstates the flexible energy by 18\% on the coldest days and understates it by 9--11\% between 11 and 15~$^\circ$C. The estimate is closest to the actual, within 2\%, between 7 and 11~$^\circ$C, around the mean temperature of the heating days, 7.4~$^\circ$C.

On the coldest days, the fitted arm runs above the measured HP load. The measured load grows by about 0.02~kW per kW installed and degree down to 0~$^\circ$C, but by half that below it, whereas the fitted arm stays linear. The energy factor reaches 1.09 on these days, and the daily resolution and the pilot's SF add 4\% and 2\%. The capacity estimate plays no part, since it drops out of \eqref{eq:flex-energy}.

In mild weather, the estimate misses the HP use that does not depend on temperature, most plausibly hot-water heating \cite{Bru23}. The fitted arm reaches zero at $T_h$, whereas the measured HP load still draws 0.05--0.06~kW per kW installed at 14--16~$^\circ$C.
\section{Validation outside the Pilot}
\label{sec:validation}

\subsection{Aggregates outside the Pilot}
\label{sec:real-agg}

Section~\ref{sec:results} tested the method within one population, on aggregates built from its households. This section applies it to aggregates that no pilot has seen, each summed from its own field meters. Swiss and German aggregates test the heating regime with the Kloten pilot. Submetered data serve only to validate the results, never as input.

Three aggregates lie outside the Kloten pilot. Two are regional aggregates: the HEAPO households of two further Swiss weather stations, Z{\"u}rich-Affoltern (Affoltern below) and Einsiedeln, with 21 and 6 submetered HPs. Their households share an area but not necessarily an LV network. The third comes from the WPuQ measurement campaign in Hamelin \cite{Sch22}. It is a real aggregate of 37 German single-family houses on one LV network, each with a submetered HP, and its net load is the sum of their individual meters. All three are summed rather than metered at a transformer, so they carry no network losses. They also contain no households without an HP, which makes their HP penetration 100\%. The pilot now comprises all 50 Kloten HPs. Its SF fit, at the pilot's own net-load threshold, gives $\hat{\mathrm{SF}}_h(T) = 0.029 + 0.018\,(16.6 - T)$, and only its sensitivity $m_h = 0.018\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$ transfers.

\input{tables/tab_real}

On all three aggregates, the capacity estimate lies within 10\% of the true capacity (Table~\ref{tab:real}), with errors of $+2\%$ at Affoltern, $-6\%$ at Einsiedeln and $-9\%$ at Hamelin, an nMAE of 3.8\%. Einsiedeln holds only six HPs, below the ten from which Section~\ref{sec:res-capacity} finds the estimates reliable, so its result is indicative. The flexibility ratio on the heating-regime days lies between 0.93 and 0.96, slightly below the Kloten median of 1.04 (Section~\ref{sec:res-flex}). It follows the energy factors of 0.89 to 0.92, while the unused-capacity factor stays between 1.01 and 1.05. These energy factors are lower than on Kloten possibly because these aggregates hold no households without an HP, whose temperature response inflates the heating arm.

The thresholds show why only the sensitivity transfers. The net-load thresholds are $16.7\,^\circ$C at Affoltern, $14.0\,^\circ$C at Einsiedeln and $13.9\,^\circ$C at Hamelin, against $16.6\,^\circ$C for the Kloten pilot. The threshold temperature varies with the insulation of the building stock, the heat released indoors by occupants and appliances, and the preferred indoor setpoint \cite{Men18}. Transferring the full pilot curve from Kloten, intercept and threshold included, gives capacity errors of $-3\%$, $-20\%$ and $-26\%$. The error of the full curve therefore grows with the distance between the target's threshold and the pilot's, whereas the sensitivity alone stays within 10\%.

With each target's own SF sensitivity, the errors are $+6\%$, $+5\%$ and $+3\%$. This error of the net-load sensitivity remains small, since every household in these aggregates has an HP.

\subsection{Transfer across Populations}
\label{sec:transfer-pop}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Error Sources and Transfer}
\label{sec:accuracy}

The capacity estimate carries two error sources. The first is the non-TCL temperature response that \eqref{eq:decomp} neglects, which biases the net-load sensitivity upwards and dominates at low HP penetration (Section~\ref{sec:res-capacity}). The second source is the pilot's SF sensitivity. Within Kloten, it matches the aggregates' own at a median ratio of 1.00, with a 10--90\% range of 0.84--1.22, so the transfer adds spread rather than bias. With each aggregate's own SF sensitivity, the nMAE is 7.1\%, against 9.5\% with the pilot's.

The sensitivity transfers within 12\% between populations that share a climate zone and a housing stock (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}). Transferring the full pilot curve, threshold and intercept included, raises the capacity error to 20\% at Einsiedeln and 26\% at Hamelin. The sensitivity differs by factors of 1.7 between heating zones and 1.65 between social and private housing, more than between HP technologies. Across the climate classes of Table~\ref{tab:cross}, it varies by up to a factor of four. This mirrors the inverse problem solved for PV installed capacity against irradiance \cite{gouveia2026pvcapacity}, here posed against outdoor temperature.

The pilot should therefore match the target in climate zone, TCL technology and housing stock. A pilot of a few tens of HPs appears to suffice. A pilot of 25 Kloten HPs reproduces the SF of the other 25 at a median $R^2_h$ of 0.91, and recovers their capacity at a median error of $+8\%$. The pilot should, however, submeter every electric heating source of its households, including backup resistance heat. The target needs only the net load and outdoor temperature that the DSO already holds.

\subsection{Limitations}

The within-population tests keep each pilot apart from its targets. Over twenty random splits of the 50 Kloten HPs, no HP informs both the pilot and a target, and no household recurs within an aggregate. Aggregates of the same split do share HPs, so their errors are not independent, but the twenty splits keep the results from resting on any single partition.

The full estimator is also tested fully out of sample, on three aggregates whose HPs never enter the pilot (Section~\ref{sec:real-agg}). Table~\ref{tab:transfer} tests the SF transfer further, on populations from five countries. These aggregates are few and all have an HP in every household, so lower HP penetrations are tested only on the semi-synthetic Kloten aggregates, which span 10\% to 100\%.

The non-TCL temperature response, which the model neglects, is measured rather than assumed away. Section~\ref{sec:res-capacity} sizes it per household, so the resulting bias is predictable from the household count the DSO already holds. Section~\ref{sec:uc-capacity} gives a correction.

The estimate gives the median flexibility at each temperature, not its day-to-day spread. With no recorded flexibility events, participation in demand response remains untested.

The aggregates outside the pilot also bound the effect of deliberate HP control. Swiss and German domestic HPs commonly run under interruptible tariffs, under which the utility may cut the HP supply during defined blocking periods. Since January 2024, {\S}14a EnWG also lets German DSOs throttle HPs above 4.2~kW \cite{Bna23}. Such control truncates the aggregated TCL peak and may bias the net-load sensitivity and the capacity estimate downwards. The accurate estimates on these aggregates suggest, however, that such control is not a visible source of error there. The WPuQ campaign predates {\S}14a, but blocking-period tariffs do not.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presents a method to estimate the installed capacity of the TCLs behind a feeder from its net load and outdoor temperature. Below a threshold temperature, the daily net load and the SF both rise linearly as the temperature falls. The ratio of the two sensitivities gives the installed capacity, and only the SF sensitivity is calibrated, once, on a small submetered pilot. The capacity was recovered at an nMAE of 9.5\% on 1260 aggregates built from measured Swiss households, and within 10\% on two Swiss regional aggregates and a German LV network, where every household has an HP. Subtracting a per-household non-TCL sensitivity, fitted on households without TCLs, removes most of the overestimate and lowers the nMAE to 7.5\%. The SF sensitivity transferred within one climate zone but not between climate zones or between social and private housing, so the pilot should match the target in climate, TCL technology and housing stock. The same fits give a flexibility potential at an nMAE of 4.8\%, overestimated by 18\% on the coldest days and underestimated in mild weather.

The method thus trades a modest, characterised error for a large gain in deployability. Its estimates are least reliable on aggregates with a handful of HPs and most reliable once tens of HPs are aggregated, which is the regime a substation presents. Where no submetered households are available, the net-load fit alone still gives the temperature-driven TCL energy that the flexibility builds on.

Four directions follow for future work. A wider and more diverse set of metered HPs, with every electric heating source submetered, would test the transfer on more populations and in the cooling regime. This paper could not evaluate cooling, since the Washington pilot of the NEEA households \cite{neea_eulr}, the only aggregates with complete cooling ground truth, has no day above its cooling threshold in 2023. Data-driven methods for HP and TCL quantification should be evaluated against the proposed estimator, to see where a learned model earns its data cost. Recorded flexibility events would allow $\Delta t_{Th}$ to be calibrated against temperature and the TCLs' response to a call to be measured. Finally, modelling the TCL consumption that does not depend on temperature, chiefly domestic hot water, would close the gap between the estimated and the measured flexibility in mild weather.
